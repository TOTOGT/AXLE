Dimensions Between the Integers
Three new chapters of Book 4 ask what it means for an object to have a dimension that is not a whole number. A curve at exactly 4/3, attractors thinner than a solid, and a set of dimension π.

A line has dimension 1 and a filled square has dimension 2. Replace an object by N copies of itself, each scaled by a ratio r, and its similarity dimension is log N / log(1/r). Chapter 1½ builds a curve from four pieces at ratio 2−3/2, which gives d = 4/3 exactly. It is not the Koch curve, whose dimension is 1.2619. Page 2 and Page 3 move one step up, to chaotic flows in three variables. Page 4 builds a set of dimension π and says what must be true of the space around it.

The curve was checked in exact integer arithmetic. No two pieces cross at any level tried, but from level 3 on pieces touch: 12, 70, 316 touching pairs at levels 3 to 5, and 5,544,270 at level 12. Touching is compatible with the open-set condition that the dimension formula needs, and that condition has not been proved.

The algebra is exact. Whether the limit curve has dimension 4/3 rests on a condition that is checked at finite levels and not proved.

A floating-point version of the crossing test gave false crossings at level 5, exactly at the touch points, and its counts were dropped. Page 5 lists what was withdrawn and what is open.

The Reading Room, Page 6, carries Part 11 of the serial, set from its source.

The one number to carry

0.3305. Touching pairs per piece on the tent curve at levels 9 to 12 run 0.3299, 0.3302, 0.3304, 0.3305, rising ever more slowly. That is below 1/3, so an earlier suggestion that the value tends to 1/3 is not supported. The extrapolation assumes the differences shrink geometrically, which is itself unproved.

Between Two and Three Dimensions
Chapter 2½ · What was computed, and what the checks changed

A chaotic flow in three variables contracts volume, so its attractor is thinner than a solid, yet it never settles into a curve. For each system the verifier integrates the equations with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step, discards a transient, and computes the full Lyapunov spectrum by the Benettin method (QR renormalization, tangent map carried alongside the orbit). The Kaplan–Yorke dimension is then read off the spectrum. It is a conjectured estimate, not a proved value, and any exponent within 0.01 of zero is counted as zero.

Two checks guard the code. For systems whose divergence is constant, the exponents must add up to it exactly. The Burke–Shaw field must be unchanged by the half-turn (x, y, z) → (−x, −y, z). All constant-divergence rows pass the sum check to four decimals, and both symmetry tests pass at 200 random points each. For Lorenz, λ1 = 0.909 and the Kaplan–Yorke dimension is 2.062, consistent with the usual values (about 0.906 and 2.06), which are quoted from memory of the literature and were not checked against a source for this chapter.

Limits of the numbers

Each exponent is a finite-time estimate from one orbit. Where two starts were tried they agree to about two decimals, and no more precision is claimed. This is not a proof of chaos for any system.

The chapter does not claim the systems share an invariant. They share a form, three variables and a smooth nonlinear flow, and each has its own computed numbers.

A Set Whose Dimension Is 3.14159…
Chapter 3½ · A vector space cannot have it; a set can

A vector space has a whole-number dimension. A set has other dimensions that measure how it fills space, and they take any real value from 0 up to the dimension of the space it sits in. A set of dimension 3.14159… needs at least four dimensions around it, and four is enough.

StepDerivation
The Cantor setLet s = π − 3 = 0.14159… Two pieces, each a copy of the whole scaled by r, one at each end of [0, 1]. The similarity equation 2·rs = 1 gives r = 2−1/s = 0.0074812…, so 1/r = 133.67… Since r < 1/2 the pieces do not touch.
Times a solidTake C × [0,1]3 in R4. At scale ε = rk the count is N(ε) = 2k·(r−k)3 = ε−(3+s) = ε−π, using 2 = r−s.
What is quotedThat the Hausdorff dimension of the dust is s (open set condition; Moran, Hutchinson) and that the product with R3 adds the dimensions (Falconer) are standard theorems, quoted, not derived.

The verifier’s output matches: log 2 / log(1/r) = 0.141592653590 = s, and 2·rs = 1.000000000000. At the level-aligned scales the exact count gives s at every level checked, 1 to 6. A straight fit across ten decades gave 0.158 against the true 0.142, which is what box counting does on a very sparse set, and the reason to prefer the exact level count.

The lattice trap

The grid constructions of Chapter 1½ give dimensions ln N / ln m for whole numbers N and m. In the unit 4-cube N can be as large as m4. The verifier found m = 37 with N = 84,460 giving 3.141593, an error near 4×10−8. It does not give π exactly: that would need mπ to be a whole number, and this check does not settle whether it can. The non-lattice ratio, 1/r = 133.67…, sidesteps the question.

Proposition, proved on paper: lattice dimensions are dense in (0, 4], with 0 ≤ d − ln N / ln m ≤ ln 2 / ln m. This is elementary and may well be known; no literature search was made.

This is a construction, not a natural object. π is not special: the same recipe gives e, 3.2 or 3.9. No four-variable flow was computed, so the chapter makes no claim about any natural set near π.

The edge of the mangroves, and then the fort
No. 11 The Ceiling
Pierre et Mademoiselle, by Pablo Nogueira Grossi  ·  See the note on fact & fiction, below

The first sign of other people was smoke — a thin grey thread rising above the canopy, ordinary and domestic in a way that struck Fermat, after five months of storms and reefs and jaguars weighing their scent, as almost unbearably moving. Someone, somewhere ahead of them, was cooking fish over a fire, entirely unaware that two ruined Europeans and a very large, very calm water buffalo were about to walk into the middle of their afternoon.

It was children who found them first — three of them, barefoot, fishing spears forgotten in their hands, staring up at the buffalo and its passengers with an expression that held far more curiosity than fear. One of them said something in a language Fermat didn't recognize, quick and bright, and ran ahead through the trees before he could think of a way to answer.

"They've gone to tell someone," Marie said.

"I imagine they've gone to tell everyone," Fermat said. "I would, in their position. I cannot think of a single thing in my own life half as interesting as what they just saw."

The children's voices carried ahead of them through the trees, rising and multiplying, and somewhere in that relayed excitement Fermat understood, with the particular relief of a man finally permitted to stop calculating, that the worst of the journey was behind them. There was nowhere left to fall. Whatever came next — soldiers, questions, the long slow work of inventing a life — could not possibly be worse than the reef, or the crew, or the open sea.

It was exactly then, with the danger finally behind them and nothing left to keep her upright but will, that Marie's grip on him loosened, and she slid sideways off the buffalo's broad back before he could do more than lurch toward her.

He caught her before the mud did — his own arms shaking with an exhaustion he'd been refusing to acknowledge for hours, his legs barely steadier than hers — and hauled her, with a groan he made no effort to disguise, back across his own lap on the buffalo's back, her head falling against his soaked and filthy shirt.

"You are not permitted to die of relief," he told her, quite sternly, though she couldn't hear him. "I did not carry us through a reef and a curse and an army of blue-clawed demons to lose you to a fainting spell."

She did not answer, and the buffalo did not stop walking, and Fermat — dead tired in every part of himself, sixty-four years old and further from home than he had ever imagined being — found the only strength he had left was in his voice, and used it, murmuring to her in a low, steady mixture of Latin and old French that had nothing to do with what he was saying and everything to do with simply not stopping.

"We have hit the ceiling," he told her, over and over, counting it out against the rhythm of the buffalo's slow, unhurried steps. "There is nowhere left to fall. Do you hear me, Marie? The descent has a floor, and we have found it, and now there is only the climbing back up."

The gates of the fort, when they finally reached them, were already crowded with soldiers who had clearly been told to expect something, though nothing in their faces suggested they had believed it until they saw it for themselves: an enormous, mud-slicked, unbothered beast walking out of the mangroves at the exact center of the afternoon, carrying on its back an old man who looked like he had not slept in a year, cradling an unconscious young woman with a tenderness that belonged to no invading army anyone had ever been trained to fear.

A young officer took one look, crossed himself, and turned to shout something back into the fort that Fermat didn't understand but recognized instantly all the same, because it was the sound every group of frightened, curious men made in every language he had ever learned: come and see this.

He straightened his back as best his exhausted spine allowed, arranged his face into the calm, unhurried authority of thirty years spent commanding a courtroom's attention, and began, in careful, formal Portuguese, to explain that he was a shipwrecked merchant, that the lady was his charge, and that he would be very grateful indeed for a place to let her rest.

It was, he thought, the first entirely true thing he had said aloud in five months, if one did not count the merchant part, or the shipwreck, or very nearly everything else. But the gratitude, at least, was real. He held onto that, and let the rest be handled, for now, by the last reserves of a mind that had gotten them this far on arithmetic alone.
