The Repair That Was Reported and Never Made
An errata told a federal agency that a defective statement had been deleted. Three days later it was still in the file. The scan that found it had been written that morning, for something else

On 18 August this desk issued an errata correcting its response to NASA’s Moon Base request for information. The errata quoted the offending line — a theorem whose entire content was True, which compiles, contains no sorry, and passes every axiom check ever written — and stated that such statements had been deleted rather than converted to sorry.

On 21 August the statement was still there. The correction had been made in a working session and never committed: the document reported the repair, the repository never received it, and both halves were written in good faith. No layer could see the gap. Not the compiler, not the kernel, not continuous integration, and not the person who wrote the errata.

A verification stack can certify that a proof supports a statement. It can never certify that a statement supports a claim — because one side of that comparison is a sentence in English.

What followed was a week of pointing the checkers at the corpus that built them. Seven distinct ways a check can pass while the claim behind it fails are set out on , with the date each was closed. Six are now mechanical. The seventh is not, and this desk does not believe it ever will be.

Of twelve theorems filed against NASA functional gap codes, all twelve are true, all twelve are proved, and five prove something other than what they are filed as. One establishes that a colony’s seed cells survive n growth steps and is recorded as a proof of reachability — the opposite inclusion. No axiom check can see this, because the defect is in neither the mathematics nor the prose but in the join between them, and every layer inspects one side.

The remedy is not another tool. It is a habit, and habits are taught: the classroom arm of this desk now runs it as a ninety-minute lesson, described on along with what the word generative is doing in this journal’s masthead, given what has happened to that adjective since 2022.

Every defect named in this issue is this desk’s own, and each is recorded in the repository with the date it was closed. The runs are public and re-runnable.

Seven Ways a Proof Can Be True and Useless
A week spent auditing this desk’s own machine-checked claims. Every defect below was found in this repository, by this repository, and is recorded with the date it was closed.

On 18 August this desk sent NASA an errata correcting its response to the Moon Base RFI. The errata quoted an offending line — a theorem whose entire content was True — and stated that such statements had been deleted rather than converted to sorry, on the reasoning that a retracted claim is not an open one.

On 21 August the statement was still in the file. A scan written that same morning, for a different purpose, printed it on its first run.

Both halves were sincere and the gap between them was invisible to every layer — to the compiler, to the kernel, to continuous integration, and to the person who wrote the errata.

The five that were filed wrong

Twelve theorems in this repository are indexed against NASA functional gap codes. All twelve are true, all twelve are proved, and every one reports only the standard axioms. Reading the statements against the codes they answer, five prove something other than what they are filed as.

One asserts that the seed cells of a colony survive n growth steps, and is filed as a proof of reachability — which is the opposite inclusion. One proves that adjacency is symmetric and is filed as proving that neighbours share exactly one interface. One proves n_layers = 6 by unfolding a definition, and is filed under a claim about processed regolith.

No axiom check can see any of this, because the defect is not in the Lean and not in the prose. It is in the join between them, and every layer inspects one side.

The ceiling, stated as a ceiling

The first six classes are mechanical: both sides of each comparison are machine-readable. The seventh is not, and will not become so.

What a verification stack can certify that a proof supports a statement and never that a statement supports a claim — because one side of that comparison is an English sentence.

What remains mechanisable is the bookkeeping, not the judgement: require every theorem cited in a claims table to carry an identifier, require every identifier in the prose to resolve to exactly one theorem, fail on either side unmatched. That catches drift. It catches nothing about whether the pairing was ever warranted.

The residue is a signed three-column table — statement as written, what it strictly licenses, what is claimed from it — and a person is the only thing that can produce it.

Closed this week

The vacuity gate. Six statements of the form : True := trivial are declared in a baseline file; an undeclared seventh fails the job. It caught one on its first run — the one the errata had reported deleted.

The axiom gate. One of the twelve rested on native_decide, an axiom emitted when a goal is discharged by compiled code rather than by the kernel, inside a step named Kernel axiom check. Rather than permit the axiom, the proof was rebuilt with decide: the kernel now reduces the goal itself.

The vocabulary gate. A search engine’s generated summary described this corpus as using a “Log-Psi Recurrence Operator” and “Harmonic Resonance Bands (HRB).” Neither term occurs anywhere in it. A 148-term declared baseline now fails on any coined term that appears undeclared.

The week’s own coincidence

On the same night, two working sessions with no contact between them diagnosed the same problem in the same file — a 168 KB instruction document, most of it closed history, loaded in full at the start of every session — and both split it, one into CLAUDE-ARCHIVE.md and one into docs/audit-log.md.

Same diagnosis, same remedy, different filename, arrived at independently. The merge that reunited them conflicted on exactly that file and nothing else.

It is a small instance of a large claim this desk has been arguing elsewhere all week: that where a problem admits few solutions, unconnected parties will arrive at the same one, and the arrival is evidence about the problem rather than about the parties.

Why print the defect list rather than the green badge: because seventeen theorems now pass a kernel check that asks for them by name, and that sentence is worth exactly as much as the reader’s ability to see what it does not cover. The run is public and re-runnable. So is the list of what it cannot see.

What Generative Science Is, and What This Opus Does
Printed because the adjective has been taken. A reader arriving in 2026 assumes we mean machine text generation. We do not, and the older sense is worth defending.

Generative here is the sense Joshua Epstein put in one line: if you didn’t grow it, you didn’t explain it. An explanation is generative when it exhibits a rule you can run, whose repeated application produces the structure you set out to account for. Not a correlation. Not a fitted curve. A mechanism, and the discipline of watching what it does.

The lineage is older than the phrase. Pāṇini’s grammar of Sanskrit, around 400 BCE, is a finite rule set generating an unbounded set of well-formed forms — and generating no others. That last clause is the whole idea. A generative system is as much a boundary as an engine, which is why this desk treats grammars, growth laws and operator algebras as one kind of object.

This journal’s addition is narrow and it is the part open to attack. Where the generative tradition builds a purpose-made model per phenomenon and demonstrates sufficiency by running it, this series claims one operator algebra across unrelated domains, establishes sufficiency by machine-checked theorem rather than by simulation, and adds what a simulation cannot supply: a rate at which deviations decay, a radius beyond which they do not, and a limit the sequence approaches. Sufficiency is a weaker claim than inevitability. We are making the stronger one, and it is correspondingly easier to break.

The ladder the volumes climb

The series is often read as running from abstract to applied. It is better read as a schedule of what each step spends.

The trade runs the uncomfortable way. Nobody’s life turns on Hille–Yosida; the delirium burden is where it would matter. Warrant weakens exactly as consequence strengthens, and that is why the tags on every claim in this series are not decoration. They record which bridge a sentence has already crossed.

What the opus actually does

Four things, and it is worth being plain about which is which.

1 · It states one algebra — four operators, compression, coherence, fold, union — and applies it in catalysis, the atmosphere, molecular biology and household finance without changing it between domains.

2 · It proves what it can. Seventeen theorems are asked of the Lean kernel by name on a public runner, and none rests on an admitted proof. That sentence is worth exactly what the reader can see it does not cover.

3 · It publishes its own defects. Every failed check, with the date it was fixed, including an errata to a federal agency that reported a deletion which had not happened. Nothing here has ever been corrected quietly.

4 · It marks what it has not earned. Five tags, from verified to held, applied per claim rather than per volume — which is the only honest way to publish a framework that reaches from a theorem to a hospital.

Five Answers Nobody Asked This Desk For
On 20 August this desk filed five Notices of Intent with NASA. No budget was requested in any of them. This page is here because the barrier to doing the same is far lower than almost anyone believes, and the agency is the party asking.

A Request for Information is not a grant competition and not a solicitation. It is an agency writing down, in public, that it does not know something and would like to be told. NNJ26ZSA003L asks: what can we learn about human health and performance from astronauts living and working on a Moon Base? That question was open. Anyone could answer it. Almost nobody does.

Where the gaps came from

Not from a laboratory. From reading, closely, twelve documents the agency published in June and left on the open web — the Human Research Program’s Risk Approach Plans, one per risk, each with its knowledge gaps, deliverables and dates.

Read as a set rather than one at a time, the distribution is informative. Behavioural Health carries nine numbered deliverables; Earth-Independent Operations, Team and Carcinogenesis carry eight apiece. Bone carries one, printed identically in both the characterisation and countermeasure columns. Venous Thrombosis splits its work into 0-G and partial-G with separate dates; EVA does the same. Bone does not — although bone is the risk where a sixth of Earth’s gravity should matter most. And there is no dust plan in the set of twelve at all.

That is a weekend of careful reading, and it produced five specific, checkable proposals. The document is public. The copy this desk worked from is archived with its checksum beside the paper that uses it.

Why you should file one

The cost is a form. An NOI is form NRESS-300.1: title, contact, dates, organisation. It says on its own face that it is not binding. Two pages.

The money barrier is imaginary. All five of these request zero dollars. You are not asking to be paid; you are offering a reading. Nothing about an RFI requires a budget, a laboratory or an institution.

The credential barrier is a registration. A UEI and a CAGE code, obtained free through SAM.gov. That is the whole gate, and it is administrative rather than academic.

The real cost of not filing silence reads as assent An agency that receives no objection records that it received no objection. Absence of comment is entered as agreement, and the people best placed to notice a gap are usually the ones who assume the room is not for them.

If you have read a public document closely enough to see something missing in it, you already have the only qualification the process actually requires.

What a Notice of Intent is not

Stated plainly, because this journal has spent the rest of the issue on claims that outran their evidence. An NOI is not an award, not a grant, not a review, and not an endorsement of anything in it. NASA has agreed to nothing by receiving one. Nobody who files one may describe themselves as working with the agency, funded by it, or affiliated to it, and this desk does not.

What it is: a filing that puts a specific, falsifiable observation into a public record on the date it was made. That is worth doing on its own terms, and it is worth doing whether or not anything follows.

The deadlines are published, the forms are free, and the questions are genuinely open. If this page persuades one reader to spend a weekend with a set of public plans and then file two pages about what is missing from them, it will have been the most useful thing in the issue.
