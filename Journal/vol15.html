Three Tests That Could Have Failed
Last issue withdrew an equivalence and left two criteria for the Riemann Hypothesis standing. This week the desk ran numerical tests of them, wrote down in advance what would count as a pass, and showed each test able to fail before trusting it.

All three passed. A search of every integer up to ten million found no violation of the arithmetic criteria of Lagarias and of Robin. The 138 zeros of the zeta function up to height 300 were all found on the critical line, and all simple. A fine grid around the first 29 zeros found the real part of ξ′/ξ positive everywhere off the line. Page 2 sets out each test and the control that had to fire first.

A pass here is a statement about a finite range and nothing more. The published verification of the zeros reaches a height of 3×1012; this issue’s reaches 300. Page 2 puts the two side by side. One control, written into the plan before the run, turned out to be too weak to detect anything; Page 4 says what was done about it.

A test that has never been shown able to fail has not yet passed anything.

Page 5 is the open desk: the Li coefficients, Weil positivity and Jensen polynomials were not tested, because no instrument was built for them.

Page 3 carries the 55-minute hour, a clock at Yale’s founding date, and a headroom check on the 8/9 paper: how far 11/12 is from its method.

None of the Lean was rebuilt for this issue. The Reading Room, Page 6, carries Part 10 of the serial, set from its source.

The one number to carry

138. The count of sign changes of Hardy’s Z-function on (0, 300] equals the argument-principle count of zeros in that range, 138. A gap would have meant a zero off the line or a double zero. There was none.

Three Tests, Written Down First
What was checked, over what range, and what each test had to show it could do

The plan was committed before any of the code ran: the data, the ranges, the pass rule and, for each test, a control. A control is a run on a world where the answer is known to be “fail”. If the control does not fail, the test cannot see what it is looking for, and its pass means nothing.

Why the margin in T3 is small

On the critical line Re(ξ′/ξ) is exactly zero, so near the line the real part is small for a reason that has nothing to do with whether RH holds. At σ = 0.5005 it should be about 0.0005 divided by the squared distance to the nearest zero, and the minimum, 0.00203, is at the edge of the window half a unit from a zero. The test cannot see a zero closer to the line than the grid spacing of 0.0005 in σ.

The title of the Platt–Trudgian paper puts the checked height at 3×1012. This issue checked up to 300, a ratio of ten billion to one. Read every pass here that way: the desk repeated a check in a small range that others have done in a vast one, with a control attached, and learned how its own instruments behave. The count of Z sign changes against the argument-principle count has no error bounds, so it is evidence in the range, not a certified verification.

A target, and how far the method is from it · 55/60

The editor asked whether the proportion of zeta zeros known to be simple or on the line can go from 0.88893 to 55/60 = 11/12 ≈ 0.9167, a gain of 0.0277. The Cobeli–Zaharescu paper was read in full and its constant reproduced from its own numbers: 0.88893.

Inside the paper’s formula, 11/12 needs the local remainder coefficient to be 2.3 to 3.1 times the paper’s. A proxy for the best that sharper local analysis could give with the same test function, built from lattice patches and not a proof, reaches about 0.8906, a gain of 0.0017. A remainder four times larger would give 0.9047, still short.

So 11/12 would need a different ingredient, not a tighter version of this one, and nothing here proves anything. The test function was not re-optimised. The clock’s 12/11 and the zero proportion share only that both are ratios: a shared functional form, not a shared invariant.

Deeper into the mangroves, past midday
No. 10 What the Cat Wanted
Pierre et Mademoiselle, by Pablo Nogueira Grossi  ·  See the note on fact & fiction, below

The buffalo slowed before either of them understood why, and it was another full minute before Fermat saw it: a shape low in the fork of a mangrove root, gold and black and entirely still, watching them with an attention that had nothing hurried in it at all.

Marie's hand found his arm without either of them deciding it should. "Pierre."

"I see it."

"What is it doing?"

It was, as far as Fermat could tell, doing nothing more alarming than a very large, very beautiful cat holding its own council, head tilted, nostrils working the humid air in slow, deliberate pulls, its golden eyes moving over the two of them with a curiosity that looked, absurdly, almost scholarly — the same unhurried attention Fermat himself might have given an unfamiliar equation before deciding whether it was worth the trouble of solving.

"I believe," he said, keeping his voice as level as six months of practiced calm could make it, "that we smell extremely strange to it."

"We smell extremely strange to everyone, Pierre, we have not bathed properly in five months."

"Precisely my point. To a creature that hunts by scent, two Europeans marinated in salt, mud, buffalo, and considerable personal despair do not smell like anything it has ever been taught to want. I suspect it is simply trying to decide what we are, in the way a man might stare at a coin from a country he's never traded with."

The jaguar's tail moved, once, an unhurried flick, and it did not come down from the root, and it did not look away, and for the length of perhaps four breaths the entire mangrove seemed to hold itself as still as the cat did, waiting to see which way the arithmetic of the moment would resolve.

It was the buffalo that resolved it. The great beast, which had not so much as broken stride at the sight of the jaguar, continued forward at exactly the pace it had been keeping since the sandbar, hooves finding the firm ground beneath the mud with the same placid indifference it had shown the crabs, the storm, the open sea — passing close enough beneath the mangrove root that Fermat could have reached out and touched the animal's paw, and never once turning its great head to acknowledge the danger at all.

The jaguar watched them go. It did not follow. Whatever question it had been turning over in that unhurried, golden-eyed way, it seemed, in the end, to decide the two strange-smelling creatures riding the indifferent giant were not worth the trouble of an answer.

"It's letting us pass," Marie whispered, once the shape had fallen behind them and the mangrove had swallowed it from sight.

"I don't believe it thought of it as letting anything," Fermat said. "I believe it simply couldn't find a category for us, and a creature that cannot categorize something is, in my experience — admittedly gained entirely from magistrates rather than jaguars — generally reluctant to commit to eating it."

Marie let out a breath that was not quite a laugh, but was closer to one than anything she'd managed since the wreck. "I have never in my life been grateful to smell this badly."

"Nor I," Fermat said, and meant it, and found himself thinking, not for the first time on this strange, drowned, buffalo-carried journey, that the whole of his life's careful arithmetic had never once prepared him for a proof this simple: that safety, out here, had nothing to do with cleverness at all, and everything to do with smelling like something the jungle had no name for.
