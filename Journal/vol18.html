A Test That Mostly Cannot Say No
A new chapter tests a way to tell two explanations of economic cycles apart. On series as long as macro data usually are, it often misses a truly two-state economy that flips rarely.

When two economic series rise and fall together, there are two ways to explain it. One shock may be moving both. Or each may sit in one of several stable states, with a pull between them that drags one along when the other flips. Both stories predict the same correlation, so the correlation settles nothing.

Chapter 19 of Book XXVI (read it here) builds the smallest model that contains both stories and asks whether a statistical test can separate them. On simulated series about 250 points long, the test found a truly two-state pair in 98% of runs when the pair flipped about eight times per series. When it flipped about once in five series, the test found it 17% of the time. The chapter had set 80% as the bar.

A series that does not look bimodal is not evidence against several stable states at this length. A single national series can neither confirm nor rule them out; panels of many series would be needed.

Also in this issue: Page 2 sets a new page on Piketty’s two laws against the book itself, and sends readers to a working paper whose sentence about the book was corrected this week. Page 3 has the toy model and a Lean file that checks its algebra. Page 4 reports that the four sets of labels used to measure the series’ tools have been read only by Claude.

The Reading Room, Page 6, carries Part 13 of the serial, set from its source.

What Piketty Claims, and Where He Claims It
A new page in Book VII pins each claim in Capital in the Twenty-First Century to the printed page. The series’ own reading comes second, and is marked as untested.

The page does one narrow thing: it says what the book states, and on which page. It begins with two equations. The first law, α = r × β, is an identity (page 52). If capital earns a 5% return and is worth six times a year’s income, capital takes 30% of income. The second law, β = s / g, says the ratio of capital to income settles toward the saving rate divided by the growth rate (page 166). Saving 12% of income at 2% growth gives six times. The book is careful to call this asymptotic (page 168): where an economy is heading, not where it is on a given day.

The contested claim is that the return on capital exceeds growth. Piketty calls it “a contingent historical proposition” (page 358), which is a claim about history and not a theorem. Elsewhere he says that divergence is one of several possible directions and that it is not caused by a market imperfection (page 27), and the book gives the r-greater-than-g relation a name of its own on page 571. The series’ page keeps to these statements and does not add to them.

The checks confirm what the book says and where. They do not test whether it is true.

A correction to read. In the series’ working paper WP-32, The Forced Urgency Gap, section 7.4 is titled “Past Piketty — a mechanism for r > g”. One sentence in it read more into the identity than the book does. It now says that the book gives reasons for r exceeding g (page 358), while the identity does not say how the excess return is collected, or why it persists for a given holder. Readers who want to judge the correction should read WP-32 section 7.4 beside the Piketty page: the working paper is where the series argues for a mechanism, and the Piketty page is where it states only what the book says.

How it was checked. A script opens the held copy of the book, a 674-page PDF in which the printed page is the PDF page minus 5, and confirms that each cited page carries the cited words. On the author’s Mac it reported 21 PASS and carries two controls built to fail. Where the book is not on the machine it reports SKIP, not PASS, so a machine without the book cannot report a pass.

What the page does not claim

The page also offers a reading of its own: that the long record looks like two regimes with shocks between them. That reading is the series’, not Piketty’s, and it has not been tested.

Two Stories for the Same Correlation
Book XXVI Chapter 19 builds a toy economy with both explanations in it, tests how often a statistic can tell them apart, and has its algebra checked by machine.

The toy has two nodes: a larger economy, A, and a smaller one, B, whose stable states are shallower and easier to leave. Each is pulled toward the other with a strength the chapter calls k, and both are shaken by noise. The one part worked out by hand is the symmetric case, with the two nodes equal. There, a state with the nodes on opposite sides exists only when k is below 1/2, and is stable only when k is below 1/3. The chapter puts the result plainly: enough coupling removes the several stable states, and a strong pull makes the pair behave as one.

To look for several stable states in data, the chapter uses a two-part test. A mixture of two bell curves must fit the series better than one bell curve by a wide margin (more than 10 on the Bayesian information criterion), and the two curves’ centers must sit more than two pooled standard deviations apart. On simulated series the test called a truly bistable pair bimodal, with a gain of 13,934.5 and a separation of 5.65. It correctly declined to flag a linear pair driven by a common shock (−29.7 and 0.64). The second condition earned its place on a third case, a cycle in volatility: the first test alone gives it a gain of 3,996.1 and would call it bimodal, but its separation is 0.00.

The harder question is how often the test works at the length of a macro series, about 250 points. It depends on how often the series flips between states.

At about 250 points, a truly bistable pair was called bimodal in 98% of series that flipped about eight times, 59% of those that flipped about 0.9 times, 17% of those that flipped about 0.2 times (once in five series), and 2% of those that never flipped.

The chapter’s bar was 80%, so the test misses it whenever flips are rare. On the control series, which have one stable state, it never fired. The conclusion follows: one national series can neither confirm several stable states nor rule them out. Panels of many series would be needed.

The algebra, by machine. The file Ch19MultistabilityAlgebra.lean states the symmetric-case results as theorems: the opposite-sides state exists exactly when k is below 1/2; its two stability numbers are −2 + 6k and −2 + 4k, both negative exactly when k is below 1/3; the same-side states have −2 and −2 − 2k. It compiled with no output on the author’s Mac on 13 November 2026, against Lean v4.32.0 and Mathlib v4.32.0, and the axiom check lists only the three standard axioms for all 14 theorems.

What this does not show

No real series was tested; the numbers come from simulations of a stated length. The model has a pull, not care or investment, and says nothing about why a larger economy would pull a smaller one. The Lean file covers the symmetric case only, not the asymmetric toy the script runs; it proves the signs of the stability numbers, not that negative numbers mean stability (the standard linearization theorem); and it says nothing about the simulation.

Who Has Checked the Labels? So Far, Only Claude
Agreement between two readings of four small label sets is high. But both readings were by the same model, and the sets that depend on judgement agree least.

The series uses small programs to read its own pages: one finds file names, one finds where a page refers to another, one files pages under subjects, and one collects the letters used as keys. To measure them, four sets of examples were labelled. All four were labelled by Claude, one session each. This week a second blind pass was run on each set by fresh Claude agents, which were given only a sheet with the labels removed. The full account is in Book XIV chapter 5, The Machines That Read Us.

On the two mechanical sets the readings nearly coincide. They agree on 149 of 150 Lean addresses (0.99) and on 85 of 94 rows of the key register (0.90). Cohen’s kappa, which discounts chance agreement, is 0.88 on the 86 key-register rows both readings call yes or no, and only 0.44 over all three labels, because almost every row is a yes.

On the two sets that rest on judgement, they agree less. The cross-link readings agree on 186 of 220 rows (0.85, kappa 0.72), and on 180 of the 189 rows where neither says ambiguous (0.95); on the 66 rows both mark yes, the two name the same target pages every time. The subject-tag readings agree on 48 of 60 (0.80, kappa 0.78), and on the field in 51 of 60 (0.85).

That order is the expected one, and it is the reason a different reader matters most for the sets that rest on judgement.

What the cross-link tool misses. On a sample of 40 chapters, the tool found all 22 working-paper numbers but only 19 of 31 mentions of people in the gallery (0.61). Most of the 12 it missed are names that appear once, because the tool asks for a surname twice. It cannot see “Chapter N” mentions at all, by construction. Separately, 187 chapters that had no subject row were given one this week, drafted by Claude and committed on the author’s instruction; the table now has 762 rows and no findings.

What these numbers cannot say

Both readings were by the same model, so agreement guards against careless labelling and not against a shared way of reading. The tags are an editor’s decisions, so “agreement” means agreement with those decisions. The cross-link sample is 40 pages out of 743.

Wanted: a labeller who is not Claude, working from the same blind sheets. Prompts for a second model are prepared, and none had been returned when this issue was set. Even a second model is another language model and not a human reader.

Told to Fermat on a step, by a man his own age, in the fifty-first year
No. 13 We Built It Last Week
Pierre et Mademoiselle, by Pablo Nogueira Grossi  ·  See the note on fact & fiction, below

The man's name was Aubin, or had been, or was the part of a longer name that had survived being said aloud in Portuguese for half a century. He was fifty-one, missing most of the teeth on one side, and he had the specific fluency of someone who has spoken French every day of his life and never once to a stranger.

Fermat had asked him — carefully, having learned in four days that careful questions were the only kind that got answered — how it was that anyone here spoke French at all.

Aubin thought this was the funniest thing he had heard in some time.

"They threw us out," he said, when he had recovered. "In the year fifteen. My mother was two. Everybody agrees about this. The Portuguese threw the French out of Maranhão, there was fighting at Guaxenduba the year before, La Ravardière went home in a ship, it is all extremely well established." He spread his hands. "And then everybody left."

"Everybody left," Fermat repeated.

"Everybody left," Aubin agreed. "Except the ones who did not."

He explained it with the patience of a man who had explained it before, though probably not in French.

An expulsion is a thing done to an army and a governor and a flag. Those are the parts that can be put on a ship, and they were. What could not be put on a ship — because nobody thought of it as French, including, largely, itself — was a few hundred people who had been living up and down that coast for three years, who had married into Tupinambá families, who had children, who had no property in France to return to and in several cases no France they had ever seen.

"So we did not leave," Aubin said. "We just stopped being anything."

Fermat asked who, exactly. He was, even four days in, still a man who wanted the particulars.

Aubin counted it on his fingers, without hurry.

"The women," he said. "First. Always first. They were Tupinambá — this is their country, senhor, they were not going anywhere, and nobody had ever asked them whether they wished to be French in the first place. A ship arrives and says you are now French. A ship leaves and says you are now nothing. The women were exactly as much of one as of the other, which is to say not at all, and they stayed where they had always been standing."

"And the children."

"The children stayed with the women. Where else." He shrugged. "Some of the fathers wanted to take them. A few asked. But you cannot put a child of two on a ship to a country where it is a bastard and nobody speaks to its mother, and most of them worked that out on the beach, and it is the one decent calculation any of them made."

He was quiet for a moment.

"And then there were the men who stayed."

Fermat had assumed — he examined the assumption afterward and did not like what it revealed about him — that any Frenchman who remained had done so out of something. Love of the place. Ambition. A grudge against the crown. Some motive with an edge to it that a magistrate could file.

"No," Aubin said, when he put it that way. "Some, maybe. My mother's husband, I think — I do not know, he did not talk about it. But mostly?"

He looked out at the road.

"Mostly they were sick and tired, senhor."

Three years on that coast, he said. Three years of fever and bad water and worse rations, of burying people, of a garrison that had stopped being paid, and at the end of it a Portuguese army and an order to get back on the same ships that had brought them — for another crossing, four thousand leagues, in a hold, at that age, with that chest, having already done it once and knowing exactly what it was.

"And a man stands on the sand and he looks at the boat," Aubin said, "and he looks at the shore. And on the shore there is a woman he has been living with for two years and a child who knows his voice and a hut that does not move under him and food he can catch with his hands. And on the boat there is five months of vomiting and then France, where he owns nothing, because he never owned anything, which is why he came."

He turned his hands over.

"They were not staying for anything. They just could not face the water again. That is the whole of it. Half of this town is here because a few dozen men were too tired to get back on a ship."

Fermat thought about a longboat pulling away into a storm, oars biting, not looking back.

Sailors, he thought, are always making this trade. Every one of them, every time. Bertrand and his crew had chosen the sea over the people standing on the wreck, and had rowed off very competently, and were presumably alive somewhere and not troubled about it.

And these men, fifty-one years ago on this same coast, had made the identical trade in the opposite direction — had chosen the people over the sea, and had paid for it with their name, their country, their language's future, and any possibility of ever being written down again.

Nobody had made a ballad about either group. But only one of them had been called a curse.

The Portuguese, Aubin went on, had been very good about it, in the way of an administration with more coastline than clerks. They took the fort. They kept the harbour. They kept the name, which everyone agreed was strange and nobody was going to be the one to raise. And then they got on with the enormous business of holding a colony, and did not, for years at a stretch, come very far up the north road.

And every so often — every few years, when a new man arrived from Belém or Lisbon with a commission and an appetite — somebody would ride out and find, in the middle of country that was supposed to contain nothing at all, a village.

"And they say: what is this."

"And you say?"

"We say it is nothing." Aubin's face did something complicated. "We say: it is very new. We say: we put this up last week, senhor, it is hardly anything, we are sorry for the trouble."

Fermat looked at him.

"A village."

"A village."

"Built last week."

"Built last week," Aubin said, "for fifty-one years."

Fermat put his head back against the wall of the step and laughed until he had to stop and hold his ribs, and Aubin watched him do it with enormous satisfaction, and a woman going past with a basket said something that made Aubin laugh too, and Fermat did not understand it and did not need to.

"They know," Aubin said, when they had both got hold of themselves. "You understand. They are not stupid men. A wall that is one week old does not have a tree growing out of it."

"Then why—"

"Because what would they do?" He said it without any bitterness at all, which was the thing that finally made Fermat pay proper attention. "Throw us out? Where? We are not French. Ask a Frenchman — you are a Frenchman, ask yourself. Am I French?"

Fermat, who had spent four days pretending to be a Portuguese merchant and had that morning been undone by a boy on a wall, did not answer immediately.

"No," Aubin said, comfortably. "So they cannot expel us to France, and they cannot expel us to the forest, because half of us are already from the forest and know it better than they do. And we do not fight anybody and we sell them fish. So they write down that there is nothing on the north road, and they go home, and the next man comes in six years and finds a village, and we tell him we built it last week, and he writes down that there is nothing on the north road."

He shrugged.

"Fifty-one years. It is the same joke. Nobody is laughing except us, and only because we started."

It was, Fermat thought later, the most elegant thing he had encountered since arriving, and he turned it over for a long time before he could say why.

They had not resisted. That was the whole of it. A resistance is a thing an administration knows how to answer — it can be counted, engaged, defeated, and entered in a ledger as defeated. What Aubin's people had done instead was decline to be enterable. They had made themselves into a quantity that could not be written down: not French, because France had sailed; not Portuguese, because no one had ever offered; not a rebellion, because they sold fish; not nothing, because there was plainly a village.

Every six years a competent official rode up the north road, applied a perfectly good instrument to the problem, and rode back with the only answer his instrument could produce, which was nothing.

I have spent forty years, Fermat thought, proving that certain things cannot exist. And I have arrived, at the end of it, among people who have spent fifty-one years proving the same thing about themselves, on purpose, to a government — and who are, every one of them, standing right here.

He had never once considered that the result might be a place to live.

"Aubin."

"Senhor."

"The fort. The one down there. That is the French fort — the original, the one from the year twelve."

"It is."

"And when they ask about that?"

Aubin looked at him for a moment with real affection, the way one looks at a clever child who has almost got there.

"Senhor," he said, "the fort is the one thing we cannot say we built last week. It is stone and it is enormous and it has their governor sitting in it."

"So what do you say?"

"Nothing at all." He got up off the step, and brushed himself down, and delivered it over his shoulder with fifty-one years of timing behind it. "They built that. Ask them. It has their king's name on it."

Fact & Fiction · the ledger for No. 13

Documented (series ledger, FACT_AND_FICTION.md): São Luís was founded in 1612 by Daniel de La Touche, Sieur de La Ravardière, and named for Louis XIII. The French were expelled at the end of 1615, after the fighting at Guaxenduba in 1614, and the Portuguese kept the fort, the harbour and the name. The ledger records a mixed French, Dutch, Portuguese and Tupinambá population as a documented consequence of the occupations.

Invented: Aubin, and the village that tells each new official it was built last week; the surname and the fifty-one-year joke are the ledger’s item 6, and the arithmetic holds from the expulsion in 1615 to 1666. Bertrand, his crew and the longboat recalled here belong to the invented voyage (item 5). The four days Fermat spent as a Portuguese merchant, and the boy on the wall, are the author’s. The figures in Aubin’s speech (a few hundred who stayed, a few dozen men too tired to sail, three years on the coast, five months at sea) are the character’s and are not sourced. That La Ravardière went home in a ship, and that the French fort still stood in 1666 as the text has it, are not checked here; the ledger documents the founding and the naming, not either later state.

“Four thousand leagues” is given as it stands in the source. It is larger than the real crossing, about 7,000 km or under 2,000 leagues (an approximate figure from the editor’s arithmetic, to confirm).
