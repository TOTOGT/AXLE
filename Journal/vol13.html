Ten Chapters on a Fifth Dimension
Book 5 gained an arc from Kaluza in 1921 to eleven dimensions. The same week, an audit of the Lean files found that most of what had been called “closed” was not.

In 1921 Theodor Kaluza showed that a fifth dimension, with nothing varying along it, turns Einstein’s equations into Einstein’s equations plus Maxwell’s. Oskar Klein added in 1926 that if that dimension is a small circle, electric charge comes in whole units. The corpus had a dimension ladder in Book 4 and nowhere to attach it to this history. Ten chapters now do that. Page 2 lists them.

The link to the series’ own geometry is one calculation, done by hand and labelled as untested in Lean. Write the fifth coordinate as y and form η = dy + κA. Then η∧(dη)² = κ² dy∧F∧F, so η is a contact form exactly where F∧F ≠ 0, which for a Maxwell field means E·B ≠ 0, and its Reeb field is ∂/∂y.

A search found this is the standard Boothby–Wang fact; the Kaluza–Klein phrasing was not found stated, and not finding it proves nothing about novelty.

The other half of the week was subtraction. Page 4 lists the corrections: a Book 4 chapter that called a rank-2 system a contact manifold, a Galilean check that fails, a theorem whose hypothesis is its conclusion, and a count of “0 sorry” that hid seven results closed vacuously. It also records where the Banach theorem actually lives.

Page 5 turns from mathematics to money: a working analysis of how a town near the city might bring visitors to eat, shop, stay and come back, using one town’s attractions and public finances.

None of the Lean was rebuilt: there is no Lean toolchain on the machine used. The Reading Room, Page 6, carries Part 8 of the serial, set from its source.

The one number to carry

Klein’s 1926 circle comes out at 8.4×10−31 cm, which is his 0.8×10−30 cm. That is the circumference; the radius is 1.34×10−31 cm, about 83 Planck lengths. A later convention gives about 23. The ratio between the two is exactly √(4π) ≈ 3.545, a choice of how charge is normalised and not a disagreement about the physics.

Contact Odd, Symplectic Even
What attaching Book 4’s dimension ladder to Kaluza–Klein showed

A contact manifold has odd dimension 2n+1, and its symplectisation has even dimension 2n+2. So the ladder alternates: contact in 3, symplectic in 4, contact in 5, symplectic in 6. Kaluza’s fifth dimension sits on a contact rung.

That has a consequence for Book 4, which had treated a 5D object with two forms as a contact manifold. It is not one: a rank-2 system of that kind is a 3-plane field in five dimensions, a Pfaffian system. The genuine 5D contact space in that setting is J1(ℝ²,ℝ), whose Darboux form dz − p1dx1 − p2dx2 equals dy + x1dp1 + x2dp2, with F = dx1∧dp1 + dx2∧dp2.

Bäcklund’s theorem: for n ≥ 2, contact transformations of J1(ℝ,ℝn) are prolonged point transformations.

That theorem replaced a Book 4 statement that did not hold. A second finding sits in the next chapter of Book 4: the Galilean prototype (y1+vt, p1+v) does not preserve the form, since its pullback is α1 + v(dt − dx), and boosts commute. Both are marked in place with a dated box.

Not yet audited: the count of 16 invariants in that chapter (6·5/2 + 1), and whether its sixth slot is Galilean time or the symplectisation coordinate.

The nine former sorrys. Read against the file’s own final status: seven were closed vacuously or by assuming the conclusion (a definition set to 0, a conclusion that is just True, a hypothesis that can never hold, a premise equal to the claim). What the repair is not. It does not show the underlying claims false. It shows that the Lean did not prove them.

Give Visitors a Reason to Stay for Dinner
Market the places people can visit now, and make each trip feed the town: eat, shop, stay, find a second thing to do, and come back.

The loop. A visitor who arrives for one thing and then eats, shops, stays and finds a second thing is worth far more than one who parks and leaves. Historic Hudson Valley’s figures for Kykuit visitors in 2025, as reported by The Hudson Independent: over half dined out, 40% visited another cultural attraction, 26% stayed overnight and nearly 20% shopped locally.

The gap this season. Kykuit tours, about 25,000 people a year, are paused for the 2026 season while the Pocantico Center is renovated. Those visitors are up for grabs, and the campus stays reachable.

Month five, and then the reef
No. 8 What the Sea Already Knew
Pierre et Mademoiselle, by Pablo Nogueira Grossi  ·  See the note on fact & fiction, below

It was Bertrand, in the end, who gave the thing its name — not out of malice, Fermat thought later, but because sailors were a people who needed every misfortune to have a name, a shape, something that could be blamed and thereby, however uselessly, avoided next time.

"There's a woman aboard," Bertrand said to the cook, low, at the rail, in a voice that carried exactly as far as he meant it to and no farther. "Has been the whole voyage, dressed as that clerk. I'd stake my hands on it."

He did not say it as an accusation. He said it the way a man reports weather — a fact now settled into the ship's understanding of itself, passed from Bertrand to the cook to the boatswain to every hand aboard within two days, and once it had passed, it did not need to be spoken of again. It only needed to be known, the way sailors had always known that a woman on a ship was a debt the sea would eventually collect, in its own time, at its own price.

They did not confront Fermat. They did not confront Marie. They simply began, in the small unconscious ways sailors have always managed a feared thing, to keep their distance, to touch iron when she passed, to mutter under their breath at a wave that broke wrong or a wind that turned sour, building — plank by superstitious plank — a case against her that required no evidence at all, only the accumulating coincidence of an ordinary voyage's ordinary bad days.

By month five, when the storm came up fast and ugly off a coast none of them had charted well, there was not a man aboard who needed to be told what it meant.

⁂

The reef took the hull before anyone had time to do more than shout it. Fermat felt the whole ship shudder the way a body shudders at a wound it hasn't yet understood is fatal, and then the deck was tilting, and the sea was coming up through the boards in a way the sea was never meant to.

He saw the crew move before he'd fully understood they were moving away — not toward the rigging, not toward the pumps, not toward any of the ordinary desperate tasks of men trying to save a ship, but straight to the longboat, hauling it over with a speed and a coordination that told him, even through his own rising panic, that this had been decided before the reef, not after it. They had been waiting, without quite admitting it to themselves, for the sea to do what they'd already agreed it would eventually do.

"Rope!" he shouted, and got no answer, and understood the answer was the silence itself.

Bertrand was the last one into the boat. He looked back once — not at Fermat, and not with any triumph in it, only the flat, closed look of a man who had already decided the matter was out of his hands and put there deliberately, days ago, in his own mind, so that this moment would cost him nothing when it came.

Then the oars bit the water, and the longboat pulled away into the storm, and did not look back again.

⁂

It was in that moment — the ship breaking under him, the trunk of silver sliding past in the flooding hold catching what little light there was and throwing it back in a thousand small, useless brilliances, his own hand closing on the smaller bag of coin because the trunk was already gone, already too heavy, already someone else's now — that Fermat felt something enormous surface beside him in the water, and did not, at first, understand it was mercy.

The beast was vast and grey and utterly unhurried, moving through the wreck-churned water the way nothing else in that storm was moving, without panic, without hurry, as though the sea's violence were simply weather it had decided not to take personally. He grabbed for its thick, coarse hide because there was nothing else left in the world to grab for, and felt Marie's hand close over his wrist a moment later, hauling herself toward the same broad back, and neither of them, in that first drowning minute, thought to wonder why an animal built for river mud and mangrove shallows was here at all, in open water, exactly when they needed it.

They only knew that the longboat was gone, small and receding into the rain, its oars pulling away from two people the crew had already decided, weeks before the reef ever found them, deserved whatever the sea saw fit to do.

The buffalo did not deserve any of their fear. It simply swam, steady and enormous and entirely unbothered, toward a shore none of them could yet see, carrying the curse and the calculation both, equally, without troubling itself over which was which.

Fact & Fiction · the ledger for No. 8

Documented, as the series ledger (FACT_AND_FICTION.md) records it; not independently re-checked here. The Marajó buffalo legend: around 1890 a cargo ship bound for French Guiana, carrying water buffalo, was wrecked on the sandbanks near Cabo Maguari, and the surviving animals swam ashore and populated the island. Importations followed in 1902 and 1906. The route La Rochelle, Tortuga, the Guiana coast, Maranhão was a functioning French Atlantic corridor in these years.

Invented, per the same ledger: the three ships of buffalo in 1666, which it calls a relocation and not a fabrication, since the real animals arrive by shipwreck 224 years later; and the voyage itself, Bertrand, the crew, the reef and the abandonment. The ledger flags that this Bertrand shares a name with Bertrand d’Ogeron, the governor of Tortuga; they are not the same person.
