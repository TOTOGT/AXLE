It collects the consolidated report on the chromium and atomic-structure calculations, the note on the local shape of zero sets at the point i, the targets for formalizing the contact geometry and related mathematics in Lean, and a plan for a volume on computational jurisprudence. What the Sources Say About Algebra and Inheritance
A premise for the new volume says algebra arose from inheritance. The sources read this week support a weaker statement.
What was read

G. J. Toomer’s entry on al-Khwārizmī in the Dictionary of Scientific Biography, read as reproduced on Encyclopedia.com; a university student page on the same book; and several posts by Keith Devlin. The Springer chapter on al-Khwārizmī’s legacy could be seen only as a preview, so it is not used. Frederic Rosen’s 1831 translation, which Toomer cites, was not read directly.

What they say

The Algebra has three parts: algebra proper, mensuration, and a third part on legacies. Toomer describes the third part as consisting entirely of solved problems, mostly arithmetic or simple linear equations, that call for knowledge of Islamic inheritance law. The preface, as quoted from Rosen, names inheritance, legacies, partition, lawsuits and trade among the uses of the book.

Three claims, kept apart

Algebra’s historical development. Toomer discusses origins as a question of Greek and Hindu sources. Inheritance does not enter.

Inheritance as an application of algebra. Supported by all the sources read: a whole part of the book is inheritance problems, and the preface lists inheritance among the book’s purposes.

Inheritance as the cause of algebra. Not found in any source read. This issue does not make that claim, and the volume’s prologue should not either.

Wording for the volume: algebra appears in the motivation and worked examples of one of the earliest systematic treatises on it, alongside inheritance, trade and partition. Gandz’s “The Algebra of Inheritance” (Osiris 5, 1938), listed in Toomer’s bibliography, has not been read and is the next source to check.

Admissible Is Not the Same as Reachable
A planned volume asks a measurable question about law: when a remedy exists on paper, what stops a person from reaching it?

The volume, Book XXXI, has a page and a provisional title, and a first-phase report sets out the plan. It builds a small ontology of legal terms from a private dictionary corpus: sense records, a typed graph, and records for rights, duties, permissions and prohibitions. No dictionary text is published and the corpus stays private.

The central definition is a pair of sets. The admissible set is what the rules of a jurisdiction, at a given time, allow. The accessible set is the part of it that a person can actually reach within limits of money, time and information. The accessible set sits inside the admissible set, and the gap between them is the object of study.

The worked case is pretrial release. Release may be legally available at every bail amount and still be reachable only if the person’s liquidity covers the premium. The share of people for whom release is available but unaffordable is an empirical quantity. No dataset has been analysed. A hypothesis has to name its data and its prediction before any analysis is run.

Old tools and new

Labelled transition systems, interval algebras for time, Hohfeld’s eight legal positions and defeasible logic are established mathematics. The report treats only the reading of admissible versus accessible as new, and says that it has no result yet.

In preparation, not published. A small executable example in which a legally available release cannot be reached for lack of money, together with a Lean statement that every accessible path is admissible. The example’s checks pass in Python. The Lean file has not been compiled. Nothing here is advice about any person’s legal situation.

What the Atomic and Nodal Notes Say, and Do Not
Three results from Revision 3, each with its limit stated beside it.
Chromium

With spin-dependent exchange switched on, the model puts the 4s¹3d⁵ configuration of chromium below 4s²3d⁴ by 2.60 eV. The ordering matches the observed ground configuration; the size of the 2.60 eV difference has not been validated against experiment. Without spin polarization the model gives a gap of about 92 meV in the other direction, stable across five numerical settings (91.6 to 92.6 meV). The ordinary spin-dependent exchange explains the reversal; it is not a result of the author’s theory. No accuracy is claimed for the energy difference. An independent code in a different basis reproduced the reference energies of helium and neon to within 1e-4 and 5e-4 hartree.

A search for a new selection rule

The idea was that which electron configuration nature picks might follow a rule other than lowest energy. The formulation tested is standard: relaxation along an energy gradient never raises the energy. For isolated atoms it either reduces to taking the lowest energy or leaves the starting point free. One zero-parameter candidate was written down before testing and was not run, because it is expected to coincide with the lowest-energy answer. No separate rule emerged.

Zeros at the point i

For a nonzero local solution of the eigenvalue equation with the stated reflection and modular symmetry, the first nonzero term of its Taylor expansion at i has even degree k and the form α·Re(wk). If the function is real and α is not zero, its zero set near i is k analytic curves through the point. This says nothing about where any function has its zeros, and nothing about the Riemann hypothesis. The local shape of zero sets of elliptic solutions is classical; what is specific to the note is the symmetry argument at i.

Earlier, Thomas–Fermi threshold values were retracted as a numerical artefact; the retraction stays on the record and a bound-state threshold is not an occupied configuration.

The north road, late
No. 14 The Children of the Sea
Pierre et Mademoiselle, by Pablo Nogueira Grossi · See the note on fact & fiction, below

He asked Aubin about the name on the walk back, because it had been bothering him since the boy on the wall, and because he had reached the stage of tiredness where a man asks the questions he has been saving.

"They call you something," Fermat said. "In the town. I have heard it four times and I cannot make it out. It sounds French and it is not being said by anyone French."

"Le Mer," Aubin said.

"That is not a name."

"No."

"It is not even correct. It should be la — the sea is—" He stopped, hearing himself, sixty-four years old, correcting the gender of an article in the only surname a whole people had.

Aubin was smiling at the road ahead.

"It has been said wrong for fifty years, senhor. By people who were not taught. It is ours now. If you fix it, it stops being ours and starts being French again, and we have been through that."

He explained it the way he explained everything, which was without any particular weight, as though it were an ordinary administrative fact and not the saddest thing Fermat had heard in a decade.

When a priest or a clerk or a sergeant with a book had needed to write one of them down — for a baptism, for a levy, for a body — the question came in the same order every time. Name. Father.

And there was no father to give. Not because nobody knew. In many cases everybody knew perfectly well, and could have pointed at a man, or at a grave, or at a horizon. But a name is not a fact about who begot you. A name is a claim on a family, in a country, backed by somebody willing to stand up and be counted for it, and there was nobody in France who was going to do that for a child in Maranhão, and the men who might have were either dead, or on a boat, or standing right there with no legal existence of their own to lend.

So the clerks wrote what they could see. Of the sea. The child came from the direction of the sea; the father came off the sea; the whole business arrived by sea and departed by sea and the sea was the only party to it that was still around.

"They meant it as nothing," Aubin said. "You understand. It was not a kindness and it was not cruelty. It was a man with a book who needed something in the second column."

Fermat walked a while without saying anything.

He was thinking about the men on the beach in the year fifteen — the ones too tired to face the water, who had looked at five months in a hold and a country where they owned nothing, and had turned around and stayed.

They had refused the sea. That was the single act of their lives that anyone would ever be able to point to. They had stood on that sand and declined, finally and permanently, to get back on the water.

And the sea had taken their children's names anyway.

"And this is why they cannot expel you," he said, at last. "I have had it backwards all afternoon. I thought the joke was that you are difficult to find. It is not that at all."

"No," Aubin agreed.

"A man can be expelled to somewhere. That is the whole mechanism — it is not a punishment, it is a transfer. You take a person out of one column and you enter him in another, and the second column has to exist, or the operation is not defined."

"Senhor, I understand about half of that."

"You understand all of it. You have been living inside it for fifty-one years." Fermat had stopped walking. "There is no France that will receive you. There is no Portugal that has offered. The forest is not a column in anyone's book. You are not a rebellion, you are not a garrison, you are not a parish. Every instrument they have is designed to move a man from one place to another place, and you—"

He was aware that his voice had gone strange, and did not much care.

"—you have nowhere to be sent. Not because you are hidden. Because there is no second column. They are not failing to catch you. They are failing to have anywhere to put you, which is a different failure entirely, and it is theirs, and it has protected you for half a century."

Aubin considered this.

"That is a great many words," he said, "for we have no country."

"Yes," said Fermat. "It is. I have spent my life on that particular vice."

They walked on.

The thing he did not say — because he had known Aubin for one afternoon, and because he was not certain he could have got through the sentence — was that he had spent four days in this town believing himself to be the strange case. The dead man. The counterexample. The one entry in the world's books that had been made and then unmade on purpose, by arithmetic, at enormous cost.

He had done it deliberately, at sixty-four, with a physician and a carter's body and three days between a death and a departure in the same port.

And here was a whole people who had been born into it, who had never once been entered in anything, who had no France to be sent back to and no name that a clerk had not invented out of the weather —

— and who had, at some point in fifty-one years, stopped experiencing it as a wound and started using it as a wall.

They are better at this than I am, he thought, walking up the north road in the dark beside a man his own age who had never in his life owned a document.

I have been dead for seven months and I am still checking to see whether anyone noticed.
