Mandelbrot's Crop Circle

Fractals, Prediction, and the Limits of Evidence

A follow-up to Crop Circles & Geometry. One more crop circle story — this one about a real mathematician, a magazine letter that seemed to predict the future, and a field in England that made international news.

CAELA Lesson · Level B (Intermediate–Upper Intermediate) · 90 minutes · Part 2 of 2

Mandelbrot's Crop Circle
Fractals, Prediction, and Reaction

Based on: the Ickleton, Cambridgeshire crop circle (12 August 1991) and the documentary The Colours of Infinity (1995, narrated by Arthur C. Clarke, featuring mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot)
A note on this story, for the teacher
In August 1991, a crop circle shaped like the "Mandelbrot Set" — a famous mathematical shape — appeared in a field near Cambridge, England. This is well documented in news archives and in the 1995 documentary The Colours of Infinity. What is not confirmed anywhere is who made it — unlike the "Doug and Dave" hoax from Part 1, nobody ever confessed to this one. It is widely believed (based on timing and location) that Cambridge University students made it, but that is an inference, not a confession. This distinction — a confirmed hoax vs. a strongly suspected one — is the heart of today's critical-thinking work, so keep it precise when you teach it.

STAGE 1 — WARM-UP / REVIEW  ·  10 minutes
Activity A: Recall Part 1

Ask: "What did we learn about crop circles last time? Who made the Doug and Dave circles? How do we know?" (Answer: two men confessed; that is strong, confirmed evidence.)

Teacher note: If this is a new group that did not attend Part 1, briefly summarize: "Some crop circles are made by humans. We know this because two men, Doug and Dave, confessed and demonstrated how, in 1991."
Activity B: Today's Question

"What happens when evidence points STRONGLY toward an answer, but nobody ever confirms it?"

Tell students: "Today's story is about a real scientist, a magazine, and a field — and about a mystery that is still, technically, unsolved."

STAGE 2 — INTRODUCTION  ·  15 minutes
Activity: Vocabulary Preview + Sentence Frames

Introduce 8 KEY VOCABULARY WORDS. Students copy into notebooks or handout:

WordMeaningExample Sentence (B-level)
Fractala shape that looks the same (or similar) at every size, when you zoom inA snowflake is a natural example of a _____.
Self-similarmade of smaller copies of the same patternThis shape is _____ — the small part looks like the big part.
Speculate / Speculationto guess about something without proofThe letter did not prove anything — it was just _____.
Predict / Predictionto say what will happen before it happensHis letter made a _____ that came true a year later.
Confirm / Confirmedto prove something is definitely trueNobody ever _____ who made the circle.
Suspect / Suspectedto believe someone probably did something, without proofPeople _____ Cambridge students made it, but no one is sure.
React / Reactionwhat someone says or does after an eventMandelbrot's _____ was calm — he seemed amused, not angry.
Coincidencetwo events happening close together, by chance, without a planned connectionWas it a _____, or did the letter inspire the circle?

SENTENCE FRAMES FOR B-LEVEL SPEAKERS (post on wall or provide as handout):

• "First _____. Then _____. After that, _____. Finally, _____."
• "This was never confirmed, but people suspected _____."
• "I think this was a coincidence / not a coincidence because _____."
• "His reaction was _____ because _____."
STAGE 3 — PRESENTATION  ·  20 minutes
Activity A: The Story, Told as a Timeline

Present the real timeline on the board, one event at a time. Read each aloud; students repeat key words.

1979–1980
Mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot, using early computer graphics at IBM, discovers and names a strange, endlessly-detailed shape: the Mandelbrot Set. It is self-similar — zoom in, and you find smaller copies of the same pattern, forever.
August 1990
A reader's letter is published in the British magazine New Scientist, joking that the ultimate crop circle would be a Mandelbrot Set. It is speculation — a guess, for fun, with no evidence behind it.
12 August 1991
A crop circle in the exact shape of the Mandelbrot Set appears in a field near Ickleton, a village close to Cambridge, England. It becomes one of the most famous crop circles ever photographed. People suspect Cambridge University students made it — but nobody ever confesses, so this is never confirmed.
Soon after
Journalists ask Mandelbrot for his reaction. He responds with something close to delight, not anger: "I am very pleased to hear of the theory taking root… I can't wait to see what the next one will look like."
1995
The documentary The Colours of Infinity, narrated by science writer Arthur C. Clarke, features Mandelbrot explaining fractals — patterns that repeat at every scale, found in clouds, coastlines, and plants.
Activity B: Documentary Viewing (Segment)

Watch a 6–8 minute opening segment of The Colours of Infinity (subtitles on). Students have a simple note-taking sheet:

What did I see?
• What is a fractal? (in your own words) _______________
• Name one example of a fractal in nature. _______________
• How did the shape look when the camera "zoomed in"? _______________
Teacher tip: Pause when the Mandelbrot Set first appears on screen. Ask: "Does this shape look like anything you've seen before? A leaf? A coastline? A cloud?"
STAGE 4 — GUIDED PRACTICE  ·  20 minutes
Activity A: Timeline Sequencing (Pairs)

Write the five timeline events (from Stage 3) on separate cards or strips of paper, out of order. In pairs, students put them back in the correct order and explain why, using sequencing language.

"First, Mandelbrot discovered the shape. Then, a magazine printed a joke about it. After that, the real circle appeared in a field. Next, Mandelbrot reacted to the news. Finally, the documentary was made."
Activity B: Confirmed vs. Suspected (Sorting)

Give students six short statements. In pairs, sort each into CONFIRMED (we have proof) or SUSPECTED (we believe it, but no proof):

  • Doug and Dave made crop circles with wooden planks. (Confirmed — they confessed and demonstrated it.)
  • Cambridge students made the Mandelbrot Set circle. (Suspected — no confession exists.)
  • Mandelbrot discovered the Mandelbrot Set in 1979–1980. (Confirmed — published, dated mathematics.)
  • The 1990 letter directly inspired the 1991 circle. (Suspected — the timing suggests it, but no one has proven a direct link.)
  • Fractals appear in real nature, such as coastlines and ferns. (Confirmed — measurable, published science.)
  • Mandelbrot was upset or angry about the crop circle. (Not confirmed — his own quoted words show the opposite: pleasure and curiosity.)
Differentiation: For lower B-level, do this sorting activity as a whole class on the board. For upper B-level, have pairs write one additional "confirmed" and one "suspected" statement of their own about the story.
STAGE 5 — COMMUNICATIVE PRACTICE  ·  15 minutes
Activity: Discussion Cards — Group Conversations

Divide class into 4 groups. Each group gets one discussion card. Students read the card, discuss, then report to the class.

Card A — Coincidence or Inspiration?

Fact: In 1990, a magazine printed a joke about a Mandelbrot Set crop circle. In 1991, a real one appeared nearby.

Question: Do you think the letter caused the circle, or is this a coincidence? Use: "I think _____ because _____."

Card B — A Good Reaction

Fact: Mandelbrot reacted with curiosity and pleasure, not anger, when a shape with his name appeared, without his permission, in a field.

Question: How would you react if this happened to you? Is his reaction unusual? Use: "If this happened to me, I would _____."

Card C — Confirmed vs. Suspected

Fact: Doug and Dave's hoax was confirmed by confession. The Cambridge students' circle was only ever suspected.

Question: Why does the difference between "confirmed" and "suspected" matter, in science and in daily life? Use: "The difference matters because _____."

Card D — Patterns in Nature

Fact: Fractals — self-similar patterns — appear in coastlines, ferns, clouds, and blood vessels.

Question: Where else in nature or in your own country might you find a repeating pattern like this? Use: "In _____, I have seen a pattern that repeats."

Group Report (2–3 min per group): Each group reports their answer to the class.

STAGE 6 — EVALUATION  ·  10 minutes
Activity: Exit Writing — Choose One

Students choose ONE task and write 4–6 sentences.

OPTION A — Retell the Timeline:
Retell the Mandelbrot crop circle story in your own words, in order, using "first," "then," "after that," and "finally."

OPTION B — Confirmed or Suspected?
Explain the difference between something that is confirmed and something that is only suspected. Use one example from today's lesson and one example from your own life.

OPTION C — Your Reaction:
If a strange, beautiful pattern with your name appeared somewhere without your permission, how would you react? Would you be angry, curious, or something else? Why?

Assessment note: A B-level student who correctly uses "confirmed," "suspected," and at least one sequencing word ("first," "then," "finally") = success.
STAGE 7 — APPLICATION  ·  5 minutes
Activity: Reflection & Connection to Life

Ask students (whole group or pairs):

  • Have you ever guessed something correctly, and then it actually happened? How did you feel?
  • Why do you think this story connects to Part 1 (Crop Circles & Geometry)? What is the same, and what is different?
  • What is one word from today that you want to remember and use this week?

"Today, you learned that not every mystery gets solved — and that 'we don't know for sure' is a perfectly good, honest answer. That is real thinking, in English."
