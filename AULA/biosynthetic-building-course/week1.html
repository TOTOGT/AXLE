Week 1 · Fundações: Conhecimento Indígena e Geometria / Foundations: Indigenous Knowledge & Geometry

From termite mounds to GTCT: how nature optimizes structure.

Learning Objectives

  • Entender arquitetura de cupins e formigas como solução de engenharia otimizada
  • Mapear estruturas naturais através do operador GTCT (Compressão-Limiar-Dobramento-Desdobramento)
  • Conectar conhecimento indígena Krenac/Maxakali a princípios matemáticos universais
  • Introduzir termologia de earth building: solos, aglutinantes, fases de transformação

Concepts

Termite Architecture as Precedent

Termite and ant mounds are among the most durable structures on Earth. Built by millions of individuals following simple local rules, they exhibit: hierarchical branching, temperature regulation, material economy, and load distribution — all without a central architect. The Krenac and Maxakali peoples of Brazil understood this millennia ago, building structures from termite-worked earth that outlast human civilizations. We begin here: nature as the primary engineer.

GTCT Framework Explained Simply

Every structure can be understood through four operations: C (Compression of function into minimal form), K (Threshold — the critical point where collapse occurs), F (Fold — recursive self-similar branching), U (Unfolding — emergence of properties from geometry). A termite mound: earth compressed into structure (C), stability tested at critical moisture (K), tunnels branch fractally (F), and life emerges (U). This framework applies to molecules, buildings, and civilizations.

Material Transformation

Earth alone is weak. Water alone flows. But earth + water + biological binders (termite saliva analogs, mycelium, clay-sand ratios) create a new material phase: a solid that is stronger, more durable, and more breathable than concrete. We call this 'biosynthetic earth.' The transformation is a phase transition — a discontinuous jump in material properties.

Why This Matters Now

Concrete and steel consume 16% of global CO2 emissions. Biosynthetic earth requires only local materials, human labor, and understanding. A single person can mobilize neighbors to build structures that strengthen with time. This course teaches that technology.

Lab Work (90-min session)

  • Visit or view (video tour) a termite mound or ant colony if possible. Sketch the branching pattern. Note: do you see repetition? Where is load concentrated? How does air flow?
  • Map a termite mound (or your chosen structure) through GTCT: where is compression visible? Where is the threshold of collapse? How does the fold (branching) emerge? What unfolds (emerges functionally)?
  • Soil collection and testing: gather earth from 3 locations on participants' property (if in course, or nearby). Test: color, texture, clay content (jar test: shake with water, observe settling rates), sand/silt ratio.
  • Introduce earth-building vocabulary in Portuguese and English. Discuss: where does clay come from? Why is termite-worked earth stronger?
  • Watch: 10-min video on termite architecture or moon-base construction (see Resources). Discuss: what surprises you? How is it similar to human building?
✓ Checkpoint · Entrega · Soil Report: pH, texture analysis, and a sketch map of your site's earth resources. Write 200 words (PT or EN): why did indigenous peoples understand earth building better than industrial societies? /

  • What surprised you most about termite architecture? How does it differ from human building?
