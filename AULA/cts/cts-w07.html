Gathering Customer Information & Conducting a Site Survey

Turning “we need better sound” into an actual spec
CTS Prep · Week 07 of 16
Course: CTS — Audiovisual & IT Systems  ·  AVIXA Ch. 11–12

Most AV projects don't start with a technical spec — they start with a stakeholder describing a problem in plain language: “we can't hear the witness from the back row,” or “the video call keeps freezing.” Gathering customer information is the disciplined process of turning that kind of statement into concrete requirements: who uses the room, how often, what's worked and failed before, what budget and timeline exist, and what constraints (historic building, security policy, IT standards) shape the solution.

A courtroom is a particularly demanding customer-information exercise: the judge, court reporter, jury, attorneys, and public all have different listening and viewing needs from different fixed positions in the room, and evidence-presentation requirements (document cameras, exhibit displays) add another layer most conference rooms never deal with.

A site survey follows the interview: physically walking the space to document what's actually there before designing anything. That means measuring room dimensions and sightlines, checking existing cabling and conduit paths, noting available power and network drops, assessing acoustics and ambient lighting (covered in depth next week), and photographing the space for later reference. The output is typically an as-built drawing or annotated floor plan — the ground truth every later design decision gets checked against.

Skipping or rushing this stage is the single most common cause of AV project failure: a beautifully designed system installed against a site survey that missed a support column blocking a sightline, or a conduit path that doesn't actually exist, creates expensive rework. The habit of documenting rather than assuming is what separates a site survey from a guess.

Job posting tie-in: The posting's line “Advise management in areas of audiovisual and courtroom technology needs, objectives, and capabilities” is this chapter in one sentence.

Key Terms

Needs assessment — translating stakeholder requirements into a spec
Site survey — physical walkthrough documenting existing conditions
As-built drawing — documentation of a space's actual current state
Sightline — unobstructed line of view from seating to displays
Scope creep — uncontrolled expansion of project requirements
Practice Questions
  1. Why is a courtroom a more complex needs-assessment case than a typical corporate conference room?
    Show answerMultiple distinct user groups (judge, jury, attorneys, court reporter, public) each have different fixed seating positions and different listening/viewing needs, plus evidence-presentation requirements most conference rooms don't have.
  2. A technician skips the physical site survey and designs a display mount based only on a stakeholder's verbal description. What's the most likely category of problem this creates?
    Show answerUnforeseen physical conflicts (structural obstructions, missing conduit paths, insufficient power/network drops) discovered only during installation, causing costly rework.
  3. What is the practical output of a site survey, and why does later design work depend on it?
    Show answerAn as-built drawing or annotated floor plan documenting actual existing conditions — every later design and integration decision is checked against this ground truth rather than assumptions.
