CTS · Week 15 · AVIXA Ch. 21–22

Conducting Maintenance Activities & Troubleshooting and Repairing AV Solutions

A systematic method beats guessing, every time
CTS Prep · Week 15 of 16
Course: CTS — Audiovisual & IT Systems  ·  AVIXA Ch. 21–22

Troubleshooting is a skill, not luck — and the most reliable approach is a divide-and-conquer method: split the signal chain in half, test at the midpoint, and determine which half contains the fault; repeat within that half until the specific failed component or connection is isolated. This is dramatically faster than randomly swapping parts, especially in a complex system with many possible failure points (Weeks 1–3's signal chain, Week 5's control logic, Week 11's cabling, all potentially involved).

Common, high-frequency root causes are worth knowing by heart because they account for a disproportionate share of real failures: loose or poorly terminated cable connections (Week 11), a device that lost power or network connectivity rather than “broke,” a control processor macro that's misconfigured rather than a hardware fault, and EDID/resolution mismatches (Week 2) that look like a broken display but aren't.

A ticketing system and clear documentation of what was tried, what was found, and how it was resolved does two things: it protects institutional knowledge so the same problem isn't re-diagnosed from scratch next time, and it creates an escalation path — knowing when a problem is beyond what should be attempted alone and needs the Audiovisual Supervisor, a vendor, or specialized support.

Because this role provides backup technical helpdesk support to end users, troubleshooting also has a communication half: explaining a technical issue and its resolution in plain language to a judge, attorney, or court staff member who doesn't need (or want) the technical detail, just a clear answer to “is it fixed, and when.”

Job posting tie-in: “Provide backup support to the technical helpdesk for end-users… receiving and responding to end-user requests… assist in implementing automation plans, maintain documentation” covers this entire week's material directly.

Key Terms

Divide-and-conquer troubleshooting — isolating a fault by systematically testing at midpoints
Root cause — the underlying reason a symptom occurred, not just the symptom
Ticketing system — documents issues, actions taken, and resolutions
Escalation — knowing when/how to hand off a problem beyond one's scope
Practice Questions
  1. A courtroom's projector shows no image. Using divide-and-conquer, what is the first useful diagnostic step, and why?
    Show answerTest the signal at a midpoint in the chain (e.g. does the source display anything on its own screen? Is there signal at the matrix switcher output before the projector?) rather than immediately assuming the projector itself is broken — isolating which half of the chain contains the fault narrows the search quickly.
  2. Why is “the device lost power or network connectivity” worth checking early in almost any troubleshooting sequence, rather than assuming a hardware failure?
    Show answerIt's one of the most common real-world root causes and is fast to rule out, so checking it early avoids wasting time on more complex hardware diagnosis for what might be a simple power/cable/network issue.
  3. Why does documenting a resolved ticket matter even after the immediate problem is fixed?
    Show answerIt preserves institutional knowledge so the same or a similar problem can be diagnosed faster next time (by any technician, not just the one who solved it originally), and supports patterns analysis for recurring issues.
