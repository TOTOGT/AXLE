Sixteen weeks, twenty chapters, one continuous thread: a signal starts as sound or light, becomes a voltage, becomes a number, travels over a network, gets routed and processed, gets designed into a system, gets physically built, gets operated daily, and eventually needs maintenance or repair. Every phase of this course built toward that full picture.

Practice Questions
  1. Trace a single signal path from Week 1 to Week 11: a witness speaks in a courtroom, and their voice needs to be heard clearly, recorded, and streamed to an overflow room. Name the general stages that signal passes through, in order.
    Show answerMicrophone (analog signal) → A/D conversion & gain staging (Week 1–2) → DSP processing/mixing (Week 3) → control system routing via matrix switcher (Week 5) → recording system capture and streaming media server distribution (Week 14) — all riding on properly designed and terminated cabling/network infrastructure (Week 3, Week 11).
  2. A stakeholder reports “the sound is bad” in a courtroom. List three completely different root-cause categories from across this course that could each independently explain that complaint.
    Show answerAny three of: poor gain staging/noise (Week 1–2), excessive reverberation or HVAC noise from the room's acoustics (Week 9), a loose/poor cable termination (Week 11), a misconfigured DSP or control macro (Week 5), a failed or underpowered device (Week 6/15), or wireless mic RF interference (Week 6).
  3. Why does this course emphasize systematic methods (divide-and-conquer troubleshooting, site surveys, commissioning, documented root causes) over just knowing individual facts about equipment?
    Show answerBecause real AV/IT technician work is mostly diagnostic and process-driven — equipment models change, but the discipline of isolating causes, documenting decisions, and following a repeatable method is what actually prevents and resolves problems reliably, regardless of which specific brand of gear is involved.
