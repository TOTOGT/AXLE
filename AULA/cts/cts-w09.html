Evaluating a Site Environment & Recommending Changes

Acoustics, lighting, and the room conditions that make or break a design
CTS Prep · Week 09 of 16
Course: CTS — Audiovisual & IT Systems  ·  AVIXA Ch. 13–14

A room's physical environment can make an otherwise well-designed AV system perform poorly — or make a modest system perform beautifully. Evaluating a site environment goes beyond the basic site survey (Week 7) into measuring and characterizing conditions that directly affect audio and video quality.

Acoustics matters most for speech intelligibility. Reverberation time (how long sound persists after its source stops, often measured as RT60 — time to decay by 60 dB) is critical in a courtroom, where hard surfaces (marble, wood paneling, high ceilings) can make speech reverberate enough that words blur together by the time they reach the back row. NC ratings (Noise Criteria) quantify background noise from HVAC and other building systems — a room can have excellent speakers and microphones but still sound bad if the air handling system is too loud relative to the target NC rating.

Lighting affects video far more than most stakeholders expect: strong ambient light (sunlight through windows, overhead fluorescents) washes out projector images and creates glare on glossy displays and camera lenses, while uneven lighting on a witness or judge creates harsh shadows that make video conferencing and recording look unprofessional.

Recommending changes is the deliverable that follows evaluation: acoustic treatment (absorptive panels, carpet, acoustic ceiling tile) to control reverberation, blackout shades or UV-filtering film to control ambient light, or HVAC modifications to reduce background noise. These recommendations often fall outside a pure AV budget or scope — part of the job is clearly explaining to non-technical stakeholders why an environmental fix, not just better equipment, is the actual solution to a problem they're experiencing.

Key Terms

Reverberation time (RT60) — time for sound to decay 60 dB after source stops
NC rating — Noise Criteria — quantifies background/HVAC noise
Ambient light — uncontrolled light affecting video quality
Acoustic treatment — materials/design reducing unwanted reverberation
Sightline — clear line of view from seating to displays (recap)
Practice Questions
  1. A courtroom with hard marble floors and high ceilings has clear individual speakers but jurors report that speech becomes hard to follow during longer statements. What environmental factor is the most likely cause, and what physical fix addresses it (not an equipment fix)?
    Show answerExcessive reverberation time (RT60) — hard, reflective surfaces cause sound to persist and blur together; acoustic treatment (absorptive panels, carpet, acoustic ceiling tile) addresses the room itself rather than the audio equipment.
  2. Video conferencing from a courtroom looks washed out and glary during afternoon sessions but fine in the morning. What environmental factor is most likely responsible?
    Show answerAmbient light — likely direct or strong indirect sunlight through windows changing over the course of the day, affecting camera exposure and any glossy display surfaces.
  3. Why might a “more AV budget” recommendation be the wrong answer to a stakeholder complaint about poor speech clarity?
    Show answerIf the root cause is environmental (reverberation, background noise) rather than equipment quality, better microphones/speakers alone won't fix it — the recommendation should address the room's acoustic treatment or HVAC noise instead.
