CAELA Guide for Adult ESL Trainers · Section III-C · Lesson Design Framework

Effective Lesson Planning
for Adult English Language Learners

The WIPPEA Model — Warm-Up → Introduction → Presentation → Practice → Evaluation → Application

Every Hour House lesson is designed using the CAELA framework (Center for Adult English Language Acquisition), based on Madeline Hunter’s Direct Instruction Model (1982). This page is the living reference for that framework — what it requires, what it means, and how it appears in each lesson.

Background · Center for Adult English Language Acquisition

What Is the CAELA Framework?

The CAELA Guide for Adult ESL Trainers (III-C: Effective Lesson Planning) is the foundational teacher training document published by the Center for Adult English Language Acquisition at the Center for Applied Linguistics, Washington, DC. It defines the professional standard for planning, sequencing, and evaluating adult ESL lessons in the United States.

“Effective lessons emerge from specific learning objectives and contain a unified set of learning activities. Learning objectives for adult English language learners are based on the needs of the students as well as existing state standards and program curricula.” — CAELA Guide, III-C: Effective Lesson Planning

The framework uses the WIPPEA model — seven sequential stages that structure every lesson from activation of prior knowledge through real-world application. Each stage has a defined purpose, appropriate grouping strategy, and activity type. The lesson objective, written in a specific CAELA format, governs the entire lesson from stage 1 to stage 7.

In Practice: CAELA to Plan, WPPPW to Deliver

Hour House lessons are planned against the full CAELA/WIPPEA standard above — objective, enabling skills, all seven stages accounted for. In the classroom, that plan is delivered as a leaner five-beat flow: Warm-Up → Presentation → Practice → Production → Wrap-Up (WPPPW). Introduction folds into Warm-Up; Communicative Practice, Evaluation, and Application collapse into a single Production beat, where students produce with the language they’ve just acquired rather than working through separately-timed stages. This is also the shape used for self-study versions of a lesson — Wrap-Up carries a short quiz, so a student working alone still gets a check on what they produced.

WIPPEA · The Seven Stages of a Lesson

The Seven Stages

Research has shown that including this series of stages in lessons will help students achieve the lesson objective. The sequence is based on the Direct Instruction Model (Hunter, 1982). A comprehension check is always included in the Presentation stage and may occur at other stages as well.

W — Warm-Up / Review
Typically 10–20 min
W
Activities at this stage make use of students’ prior knowledge to preview or review material related to the lesson.

Purpose: Activate prior knowledge; establish communicative context; generate interest before new material is introduced.

I — Introduction
Typically 10 min
I
The instructor establishes the purpose of the lesson by focusing students’ attention on it — for example, by asking questions or using visuals.

Purpose: Focus attention; state the lesson objective; motivate students for new content.

P — Presentation
Typically 20–25 min
P
New language and concepts are taught, and comprehension of the new information is checked. A comprehension check is always required at this stage.

Purpose: Deliver new content; model the target language; check understanding before practice begins.

GP — Guided Practice
Typically 20–30 min
GP
This stage provides highly structured activities that help students work with the new language. The teacher supports and scaffolds throughout.

Purpose: Controlled practice; build accuracy and confidence before open interaction.

CP — Communicative Practice
Typically 15–20 min
CP
Activities at this stage allow students to integrate the new language with their previously acquired language, usually in an interactive setting.

Purpose: Fluency over accuracy; real interaction; integrate new and old language.

E — Evaluation
Typically 15–20 min
E
The teacher and students assess students’ achievement of the lesson objective. Evaluation must match the language skill in which the objective was taught.

Purpose: Evidence of learning; performance-based or written; directly tied to the stated objective.

A — Application
Typically 15 min
A
Tasks at this stage reflect real-life applications of the lesson objective. Students demonstrate what they have learned in a context that mirrors the world outside the classroom.

Purpose: Transfer to real life; connect the lesson to students’ actual communication needs; extend beyond the classroom.

Lesson Objectives · The CAELA Format

Writing a Meaningful Lesson Objective

A meaningful objective for an adult ESL class identifies the context in which a specific communicative task will be accomplished. It focuses on one or two target language-skill proficiencies (listening, speaking, reading, writing) and states how achievement will be evaluated. The objective governs the entire lesson.

The CAELA Objective Template
Students will be able to [communicative task]
in order to [real-life purpose]
as evidenced by [evaluation method].
Example (health): “The student will be able to describe common health problems in order to talk to a medical practitioner as evidenced by his/her use of language in role plays completed in class.”

Example (Hour House / WorldQuant): “Students will be able to discuss competing theories about an unsolved historical mystery using evidence from multiple sources, in order to participate in academic conversations and evaluate arguments critically, as evidenced by a structured group discussion and a written evaluation of one theory using hedging language appropriately.”

Four elements of a meaningful objective — every objective should have all four:

C
Context — The real-world situation in which the language will be used (at the doctor’s office, on a job application, in a class discussion).
CT
Communicative Task — What the student will actually do with the language (describe, explain, write, discuss, report, evaluate).
P
Language Skill Proficiency — The primary skill being developed: listening, speaking, reading, or writing. Dictates which enabling skills must be taught.
E
Evaluation Method — How achievement will be demonstrated. Must match the skill: speaking objectives are evaluated by speaking (role plays, presentations), not pen-and-paper tests.

Common objective writing errors — these fail the CAELA standard:

✗
“Students will study vocabulary on page 45.” — Describes a task, not a communicative outcome. No context, no evidence.
✗
“Students will learn about the Voynich Manuscript.” — Describes knowledge, not communicative performance. “Learn about” cannot be evaluated.
✗
“Students will understand hedging language.” — “Understand” is not observable. No real-life purpose, no evaluation method.
Five Components · Every Effective Lesson Plan

The Five Components of an Effective Lesson

Once teachers know students’ language needs and background, they can begin to plan. These five components are found in most effective lesson plans, regardless of which lesson planning model is used.

Component 1

Topic

Communicative or real-life contexts gleaned from student needs assessments. Topics should reflect situations in which students actually need to understand, speak, read, or write English.

Component 2

Lesson Objective

The goal for the lesson. A well-written objective tells what students will be able to do — not what they will know — by the end of the lesson. Written in the CAELA format above.

Component 3

Enabling Skills

The vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation that support the student’s ability to accomplish the lesson objective. These must be explicitly taught or reviewed if not already acquired.

Component 4

Sequence of Stages

The WIPPEA progression: Warm-Up, Introduction, Presentation (with comprehension check), Guided Practice, Communicative Practice, Evaluation, Application. Each stage moves students toward the objective.

Component 5

Materials, Equipment & Technology

Everything needed to execute the lesson must be identified and secured before class. This includes realia, visual aids, handouts, textbooks, projectors, and — in the Hour House programme — Claude (claude.ai), the WorldQuant Brain simulator, and the AXLE repository.

Activity Types · Stage-by-Stage Examples

What Activities Fit Each Stage?

The CAELA guide provides a complete activity matrix across all four language skills. Below is the speaking skill example, which maps directly to the structure used in Hour House lessons.

Stage Speaking Activity Example What Students Do
Warm-Up Peer Dictation Students take turns dictating words or phrases from previous lessons related to today’s topic.
Introduction Teacher Question / Visual Teacher focuses attention on the lesson topic using a question, picture, or real-life object.
Presentation Model Dialogue Teacher demonstrates the target language, highlighting intonation, rhythm, and meaning. Comprehension checked.
Guided Practice Substitution Drill Students work with parts of the dialogue, substituting new language or transforming grammar structures.
Communicative Practice Information Gap Partners ask and answer questions using the lesson structure, filling in missing information from each other.
Evaluation Role Play Students perform a conversation using the dialogue structure with their own ideas, without the model in front of them.
Application Authentic Task Students use the language in a real-world scenario: a phone call, a job interview, a presentation, a written submission.
