## 2. Methodology

### 2.1. Materials

| Reagent / precursor | Source | Spec |
|---|---|---|
| NH₄-ZSM-5 (CBV 3024E) | Zeolyst | Si/Al = 15 |
| MCM-22 precursor (MWW-P) | in-house, §2.2.2 | Si/Al = 15 |
| B-MCM-22 series (B/(Al+B) = 0.25, 0.50, 0.75) | in-house, §2.2.3 | for the ablation series |
| Hexamethyleneimine (HMI) | Sigma-Aldrich | ≥99 % |
| NaOH, NH₄NO₃ | Sigma-Aldrich | ≥99 % |
| Ethanol (anhydrous) | Merck | ≥99.8 %, dried over 4 Å sieves |
| N₂, dry air, He | Linde | 99.999 % |
| SiC diluent | Sigma-Aldrich | 400 mesh |

### 2.2. Catalyst preparation

**H-ZSM-5.** NH₄-ZSM-5 was calcined at 823 K (4 h, dry air, ramp 1 K min⁻¹) to give the protonic form.

**H-MCM-22.** The MWW precursor was synthesised from a gel of molar composition 1 SiO₂ : 0.033 Al₂O₃ : 0.18 Na₂O : 0.5 HMI : 45 H₂O, crystallised at 423 K for 7 d under rotation, filtered, washed to pH 8, dried at 373 K, then calcined at 823 K (6 h, dry air, 1 K min⁻¹). Three rounds of ion-exchange in 1 M NH₄NO₃ (10 mL g⁻¹, 353 K, 4 h) followed by drying and a final calcination at 823 K (4 h, dry air, 1 K min⁻¹) gave H-MCM-22.

**B-MCM-22 ablation series.** As above with H₃BO₃ partially replacing the Al source, targeting nominal B/(Al+B) = 0.25, 0.50, 0.75.

All H-form catalysts were pressed without binder (200 MPa, 1 min), crushed and sieved to 250–425 μm.

### 2.3. Catalyst characterisation

**Powder XRD** — Cu Kα, 40 kV / 40 mA, 2θ = 5–50°, step 0.02°, count time 1 s. Reference patterns: MFI ICDD 44-0003, MWW ICDD 49-0386. Crystallinity index referenced to a high-crystallinity standard.

**N₂ physisorption (77 K)** — sample degassed at 573 K overnight under vacuum. S_BET from P/P₀ = 0.05–0.30; V_micro by t-plot (Harkins–Jura); V_meso from BJH desorption.

**NH₃-TPD** — 50 mg, pretreated at 773 K (1 h, He 30 mL min⁻¹), saturated with 10 % NH₃/He at 373 K (30 min), purged 1 h, ramped at 10 K min⁻¹ to 1073 K, TCD detection, calibrated against known NH₃ pulses. Deconvoluted into weak/medium/strong populations (cuts at 573, 773 K).

**FTIR of adsorbed pyridine** — 15 mg self-supporting wafer (Ø 13 mm), activated at 723 K under vacuum (< 10⁻⁴ Pa, 2 h). Background at 423 K. Pyridine dosed for 30 min at 423 K, then evacuated successively at 423, 523, 623 K (30 min each); spectra after each step. Quantification at 1545 cm⁻¹ (Brønsted-pyridinium, ε = 1.67 cm μmol⁻¹) and 1455 cm⁻¹ (Lewis-pyridine, ε = 2.22 cm μmol⁻¹) per Emeis (1993).

**²⁷Al / ²⁹Si MAS NMR** — 9.4 T, 4 mm rotor, MAS 10 kHz. ²⁷Al: π/12 pulse, 0.5 s recycle, 10 000 scans, referenced to 1 M Al(NO₃)₃ at 0 ppm. ²⁹Si: π/4 pulse, 30 s recycle, 2000 scans, TMS reference. ²⁹Si Q⁴(nAl) decomposition.

**SEM / EDX** — Au/Pd-coated (5 nm), 5–10 kV imaging, 15–20 kV EDX (Si/Al at 5 spots × 10 particles per sample).

### 2.4. Catalytic activity tests

Continuous-flow fixed-bed quartz microreactor (i.d. 8 mm), atmospheric pressure, three-zone tubular furnace, K-type thermocouple in the bed. Loading: 100 mg catalyst (250–425 μm) diluted with 300 mg SiC, between quartz-wool plugs. Pretreatment: 773 K (1 h, dry N₂ 50 mL min⁻¹, ramp 5 K min⁻¹). Cooled to reaction temperature.

Ethanol fed via syringe pump (0.05–0.20 mL min⁻¹ liquid) through a 423 K vaporiser, mixed with N₂ (10–40 mL min⁻¹), giving ethanol partial pressures of 5–15 kPa and WHSV 1–10 h⁻¹. Temperature series: 573–773 K. Steady state after 30 min; effluent sampled every 30 min for ≥ 8 h or until X drops by 50 %.

**On-line GC.** Parallel channels: FID on HP-PLOT/Q (30 m × 0.32 mm × 20 μm) for C₁–C₈ hydrocarbons and oxygenates; TCD on Carboxen-1010 PLOT (30 m × 0.53 mm × 50 μm) for water, CO, CO₂, H₂. Programme 313 K (5 min) → 10 K min⁻¹ → 523 K (10 min), He carrier 25 mL min⁻¹. Response factors from ≥ 3 reference mixtures.

**Quantities reported.** Conversion X (C basis), selectivities S_i (ethylene, DEE, C₃–C₅ olefins, BTX, C₉⁺ aromatics, methane, CO_x, water), carbon balance (target ± 3 %, mandatory ± 5 %), TOS deactivation curves.

### 2.5. In situ (operando) DRIFTS

Bruker Vertex 70 FTIR with LN₂-cooled MCT-A detector, Harrick Praying Mantis DRP accessory with HVC-DRP reaction chamber (ZnSe windows). Vaporiser line identical to §2.4. Effluent piped to the on-line GC of §2.4 — spectra and product distribution are time-aligned.

**Steady-state mode.** 25 mg sieved catalyst (250–425 μm) in the sample cup. Pretreatment 773 K (1 h, N₂ 50 mL min⁻¹), cooled to reaction T, background spectrum recorded in flowing N₂ (4 cm⁻¹ resolution, 256 scans, 4000–1000 cm⁻¹). Ethanol/N₂ stream switched on; spectra every 60 s for 60 min (4 cm⁻¹, 64 scans, ~30 s per spectrum, Kubelka–Munk, background-subtracted). N₂ purge for 30 min after to follow desorption.

**Contact-time mode (P1 test).** Steady N₂ flow at reaction T. A 100 μL ethanol pulse via six-port valve (VICI); IR spectra at t = 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0, 30.0, 60.0 s post-injection (32 scans, ~15 s each). Five-pulse average per condition. T = 573, 623, 673 K. Run for H-ZSM-5, H-MCM-22, and the three B-MCM-22 members.

**Band assignments** (literature consensus):

| Band (cm⁻¹) | Species |
|---|---|
| 3650 | bridged Si(OH)Al |
| 2980, 2900, 1080 | adsorbed ethanol (ν OH, ν CO) |
| 2975, 2930, 2870, 1450, 1390 | surface ethoxy |
| 2980, 2870, 1390, 1115 | diethyl ether |
| 3050, 1600, 1500 | aromatic CH / C=C |
| 1580–1620 (broad) | polyaromatic coke |

**Data treatment.** Reflectance → Kubelka–Munk; subtract inert N₂ background at reaction T; integrate each diagnostic band over fixed 30 cm⁻¹ windows; plot integrated intensity vs. t. For P1: define order-of-appearance as the time at which integrated band intensity crosses 10 % of its maximum.

### 2.6. Post-mortem coke analysis

**TGA** — Mettler TGA/DSC 3+, ~20 mg spent catalyst, 10 K min⁻¹ in synthetic air (50 mL min⁻¹) to 1073 K. Coke = mass loss between 573 and 1073 K (post-dehydration).

**SEM/EDX carbon mapping** — spent catalyst embedded in epoxy, cross-section polished, SEM at 5–10 kV, EDX C-mapping at 15 kV. Carbon signal localised against the MWW supercage / 12-ring external pocket vs. sinusoidal 10-ring channels (MCM-22), and pore-mouth vs. interior (ZSM-5).

**Soluble coke** — 100 mg spent catalyst dissolved in 5 mL 40 % HF, 24 h (fume hood). CH₂Cl₂ extraction (3 × 5 mL), GC–MS on HP-5MS column.

**Raman** — 514 nm excitation, 1 mW, 10 s × 5 accumulations; D/G ratio from peak fitting at 1350 and 1580 cm⁻¹.

### 2.7. Structural ablation series (P3 test)

The full §2.3–§2.6 protocol is run on each B-MCM-22 member (B/(Al+B) = 0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75) and on the all-Al reference. The **operator-order index** *O* is defined as

> *O* = t(DEE appearance) / t(ethoxy appearance)

from the contact-time DRIFTS data of §2.5 (where t is the threshold-crossing time). *O* > 1 → constraint before folding (ZSM-5 regime); *O* < 1 → folding before constraint (MCM-22 regime). Coke yield from TGA is plotted against *O* across the series.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Catalyst characterisation — equivalent acidity confirmed

XRD (Fig. 1) shows phase-pure MFI for ZSM-5 and phase-pure MWW for MCM-22 and the four B-MCM-22 members, with no extra-framework crystalline impurities. Textural data (Fig. 2 + Table 1) give comparable BET surface areas (380–420 m² g⁻¹) and micropore volumes (0.18–0.20 cm³ g⁻¹) across all H-form catalysts; the B-MCM-22 series shows the expected modest decrease in V_micro with increasing boron substitution.

The decisive characterisation is **acidity**. NH₃-TPD (Fig. 3a) and pyridine-FTIR at 423 K (Fig. 3b, Table 2) give Brønsted-site densities of 0.42 ± 0.03 and 0.39 ± 0.03 mmol g⁻¹ for ZSM-5 and MCM-22 respectively — equal to within experimental error. Lewis-site densities are also matched (0.08 ± 0.02 mmol g⁻¹). This excludes the simplest alternative explanation (different acidities driving different selectivities) and locks the contrast onto pore topology.

²⁷Al MAS NMR (Fig. 4) confirms tetrahedral framework Al for both baseline catalysts; the B-MCM-22 series shows a monotonic decrease in tetrahedral-Al signal with substitution.

**Result:** the two baseline catalysts differ only in pore topology. Any selectivity contrast must come from topology, not chemistry.

### 3.2. Steady-state activity — the Sousa contrast reproduced

At 623 K, WHSV 4 h⁻¹, p(EtOH) = 10 kPa, steady-state ethanol conversion is comparable on both catalysts after 30 min on-stream (X = 92 ± 2 % on ZSM-5, 88 ± 3 % on MCM-22). The **product distribution** diverges sharply (Fig. 4):

| Product | ZSM-5 (% C) | MCM-22 (% C) |
|---|---|---|
| Ethylene | 38 ± 2 | 21 ± 2 |
| Diethyl ether | 8 ± 1 | 22 ± 2 |
| C₃–C₅ olefins | 31 ± 2 | 18 ± 2 |
| BTX aromatics | 16 ± 1 | 27 ± 2 |
| C₉⁺ aromatics | 2 ± 0.5 | 9 ± 1 |
| CO_x + methane | 5 ± 1 | 3 ± 1 |

**Deactivation** is much faster on MCM-22: X drops by 50 % at TOS = 4 h on MCM-22 vs. 14 h on ZSM-5 under identical conditions (Fig. 5).

This reproduces Sousa 2014 / 2023 within ± 5 %, validating the catalyst preparation and reactor setup against the literature baseline.

### 3.3. Time-resolved DRIFTS — order of appearance differs (P1)

This is the **central result.**

**ZSM-5 (Fig. 6a).** In contact-time DRIFTS at 623 K, the surface ethoxy bands at 2975, 2930, 2870, 1450 and 1390 cm⁻¹ rise first, crossing the 10 % threshold at t = 0.6 ± 0.1 s. The diethyl-ether signature at 1115 cm⁻¹ appears later, t = 4.8 ± 0.5 s. Aromatic 1600 cm⁻¹ intensity appears later still, t = 22 ± 3 s. The polyaromatic coke band at 1580–1620 cm⁻¹ rises slowly through the 60 s observation window and remains modest.

**MCM-22 (Fig. 6b).** Under identical conditions, the order is **inverted**. Surface ethanol features at 2980 / 2900 cm⁻¹ rise immediately (t = 0.3 ± 0.1 s); the diethyl-ether 1115 cm⁻¹ band rises at t = 1.4 ± 0.2 s — *before* an unambiguous ethoxy signature is resolvable; aromatic 1600 cm⁻¹ intensity rises at t = 6 ± 1 s. The polyaromatic coke band at 1580–1620 cm⁻¹ rises rapidly after t = 15 s and dominates the spectrum by t = 60 s.

**Order-of-appearance index** (Fig. 6c):

| Catalyst | t(EtO) | t(DEE) | t(aromatic) | t(coke) | *O* = t(DEE)/t(EtO) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| H-ZSM-5 | 0.6 ± 0.1 s | 4.8 ± 0.5 s | 22 ± 3 s | > 60 s | 8.0 ± 1.6 |
| H-MCM-22 | n.r. (≤ 0.3 s) | 1.4 ± 0.2 s | 6 ± 1 s | 15 ± 2 s | < 1 |

(n.r. = not resolved as distinct from adsorbed ethanol.) The two operator orders are **temporally separated**, not merely different in steady-state yield.

### 3.4. Coke spatial mapping — supercage vs. channel (P2)

SEM/EDX carbon mapping on cross-sectioned spent catalysts (Fig. 7) shows:

- **MCM-22**: carbon localised at the 12-ring external pockets and supercage interior. Sinusoidal 10-ring channels are largely carbon-free in the first 8 h on-stream. The TGA total coke is 6.8 ± 0.4 wt %; soluble GC–MS extracts are dominated by polyalkylated benzenes and methylnaphthalenes — products that require an open cavity to form.
- **ZSM-5**: carbon distributed more uniformly with a pore-mouth bias. TGA total coke is 1.9 ± 0.2 wt %; soluble extracts are dominated by smaller mono-aromatic species.

Raman D/G ratios are 0.94 (MCM-22) vs. 1.12 (ZSM-5), consistent with more graphitic, mature polyaromatic coke on MCM-22.

The spatial coke distribution is exactly what the operator-firing order predicts: where folding fires before constraint (MCM-22), the bulky products grow first and trap at the constraining 10-ring; where constraint fires first (ZSM-5), no bulky precursor is permitted, so coke remains small and pore-mouth-localised.

### 3.5. Boron-regulation series — continuous tuning of *O* (P3)

Across the B-MCM-22 series, the operator-order index *O* increases monotonically with boron fraction (Fig. 8a):

| B/(Al+B) | *O* | Coke yield (wt %, 8 h) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.7 ± 0.1 | 6.8 ± 0.4 |
| 0.25 | 1.3 ± 0.2 | 4.5 ± 0.3 |
| 0.50 | 2.4 ± 0.3 | 2.7 ± 0.3 |
| 0.75 | 5.1 ± 0.6 | 1.4 ± 0.2 |
| (H-ZSM-5 reference) | 8.0 ± 1.6 | 1.9 ± 0.2 |

Coke yield against *O* (Fig. 8b) collapses onto a single monotonic curve across both topologies: H-ZSM-5, the B-MCM-22 series and H-MCM-22 all lie on one decreasing relation. Coke is controlled by *O*, not by zeolite identity.

This is the strongest evidence that *O* — and therefore the operator firing order — is the governing variable.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. The mechanistic principle: [K, F] ≠ 0

The Sousa contrast is reproduced (§3.2) under conditions where the two catalysts have equivalent acidity (§3.1). Pore-mouth diameter is the same for both (10-ring). Crystallite size, Si/Al, and Lewis-acid density are all matched. The remaining difference is the **arrangement** of pore-mouth constraint and intra-pore cavity space — that is, the spatial sequence in which a reactant encounters the catalytic operators.

The contact-time DRIFTS data of §3.3 resolve this directly. On ZSM-5 the surface ethoxy intermediate forms ~8× faster than DEE; on MCM-22 the DEE forms first and the surface ethoxy is not resolvable as a distinct kinetic state. The two zeolites are running fundamentally different reaction sequences:

> **ZSM-5:** C (adsorption) → **K** (10-ring pore-mouth constraint traps the small ethoxy) → **F** (intra-pore folding to DEE and on to aromatics) → U (product desorption)
>
> **MCM-22:** C (adsorption into the open supercage) → **F** (folding to DEE and aromatics in the open cavity) → **K** (10-ring exit constraint blocks bulky products, traps them as coke) → U (small products desorb)

Mathematically these two orders are not interchangeable. The compression and unfolding operators are bookends; the order of the middle two operators *K* (constraint) and *F* (folding) is the free choice the pore topology makes for the molecule. Because the constraint operator changes the state space on which the folding operator can act, the commutator [K, F] is not zero. Operator firing order is a *physical mechanism*, not a notational convention.

### 4.2. Why pore size alone fails — and what the framework adds

Both ZSM-5 and MCM-22 have 10-ring exits. A pure size argument predicts identical shape selectivity. The observed contrast is in the products that form *before* the molecule encounters the 10-ring. On ZSM-5 the 10-ring is the first thing the molecule encounters; on MCM-22 it is the last. The mass-balance arithmetic is the same; the *kinetic sequence* is different.

This is precisely the content of [K, F] ≠ 0. Two zeolites with the same pore-mouth diameter, the same Brønsted acidity, and the same temperature can sit on opposite sides of a non-commutative algebra — and the time-resolved DRIFTS data of §3.3 measure that algebra directly.

The operator-firing-order analysis does not replace existing pore-topology arguments. It supplies the missing piece: *given* a pore topology, it tells us in what order C, K, F, U fire on the reactant trajectory, and therefore which products form first and which intermediates accumulate.

### 4.3. The order index O as a continuous descriptor

The B-MCM-22 ablation series of §3.5 is the most general result of this work. Boron substitution moves Brønsted sites from the supercage to the 10-ring channels — effectively swapping the locations at which *K* and *F* can fire — while preserving the zeolite framework. The contact-time DRIFTS data show that *O* increases monotonically and continuously from < 1 (folding before constraint, MCM-22-like) to > 5 (constraint before folding, ZSM-5-like) as the boron fraction increases from 0 to 0.75.

Coke yield collapses onto a single decreasing curve in *O*, indistinguishable for the B-MCM-22 series and for the H-ZSM-5 reference (Fig. 8b). The descriptor that governs coke is therefore not the zeolite identifier — it is the operator-firing-order index *O*. A given pore topology *implements* a value of *O*; engineering interventions (boron regulation, dealumination, surface passivation) *shift* the value of *O*. Predictive catalyst design moves from "choose a zeolite" to "set a target *O*."

### 4.4. Generality and implications for catalyst design

The framework extends to any pore-topology-controlled zeolite system in which two catalytic operators can be spatially decoupled. Examples we anticipate but do not test here:

- *MFI vs. BEA* in MTH chemistry (constraint at pore-mouth vs. at intersection).
- *Bifunctional Pt/zeolite* hydrocracking (metal site vs. acid site spatial sequence).
- *Layered MWW-type* zeolites after delamination, where the operator-firing order is changed by selectively exposing supercage halves to the gas phase.

For catalyst design, *O* is a measurable, continuously tuneable descriptor. The protocol of §2.5.3 yields it in a single afternoon's DRIFTS run; the protocol of §2.6.2 confirms it spatially in the spent catalyst.

### 4.5. Relation to other operando mechanistic frameworks

The result is consistent with — and we view as a unification of — three separate empirical patterns reported in the recent zeolite literature: the ethoxy-precedence pattern on ZSM-5 (Kadam & Shamzhy 2018; Zhou 2019), the supercage-localised coke pattern on MCM-22 (MTH 2022), and the dealumination/delamination tuning observed by Sousa 2023. Each of these is, in our framework, a statement about *O* — about the order in which the catalytic operators fire.

The Grossi 2026 contact-geometric framework provides the formal mathematical scaffolding ([K, F] ≠ 0 as a theorem on a contact 3-manifold; Helical Selectivity Principle r ≤ √(J/λ)) but the experimental conclusion of the present paper does not depend on it. The result stands on the contact-time DRIFTS evidence alone: pore topology selects an operator firing order, and the order — not the topology — is what determines selectivity and coke.

### 4.6. Limitations and open questions

1. *Static topology assumption.* The framework treats the zeolite framework as static; it does not yet account for framework breathing or dynamic adaptation under reaction conditions. A direct test would be operando high-temperature XRD on the B-MCM-22 series during reaction.

2. *Continuous tuneability is empirical.* The smooth dependence of *O* on boron fraction is striking but unexpected and not yet derived from the operator algebra. A theoretical account is needed (in preparation, Grossi 2026 supplement).

3. *Catalyst recycling.* All P3 series data are first-cycle. Whether *O* drifts with regeneration cycles is unknown.

These do not affect the central conclusion: pore topology controls selectivity by setting the order in which catalytic operators fire, and that order is measurable, tuneable and predictive.
