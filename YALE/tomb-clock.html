What it is doing

The second and the minute are untouched — a minute here is a real minute. Only the hour is redefined as 55 minutes instead of 60. The minute hand sweeps at ordinary speed but wraps at 55, and the hour hand completes its twelve-hour revolution in 12 × 55 = 660 minutes, which is 11 real hours.

That ratio is the whole object: 60/55 = 12/11 ≈ 1.0909. Two different "gains" fall out of it, and they are easy to conflate. The readout rolls its hour over five minutes early, so read naively as h:mm it advances 1h05m per real hour. The hour hand is the honest measure: it sweeps 1/11 of a revolution per real hour against the ordinary 1/12, so it gains 1/132 of a revolution — 60/11 ≈ 5 min 27 s — every hour, and 1440/11 ≈ 2 h 11 m a day.

It cannot be a day system. 1440 = 2⁵ · 3² · 5 has no factor of 11, and 55 = 5 · 11, so no whole number of 55-minute hours tiles a day — 26.18… of them fit. It works only as a rate, running against a reference clock, which is what the five-minutes-ahead story actually describes.

The hour hands align again when that gap closes a whole revolution: 1 ÷ (1/11 − 1/12) = 132 hours — five and a half days. Not six: that figure comes from dividing 720 by the readout's five minutes, which measures the wrong thing.

Offset mode is the rival reading: a clock set five minutes ahead and left alone. It never drifts, and it is not a 55-minute hour at all. The toggle exists because the two get conflated — one is a rate, the other a constant.

Reckoning from the charter

The Collegiate School was chartered on 9 October 1701. A Connecticut clerk would have written that the 9th of the 8th month — before 1752 the legal year began on 25 March, so March was the first month and October the eighth. It is why the name means eighth, and why September, November and December mean seventh, ninth and tenth. Under Old Style the names and the numbers still agreed.

Counting forward from that day is not subtraction. England and its colonies kept the Julian calendar until 1752, then struck eleven days out of September to catch up: Wednesday the 2nd was followed by Thursday the 14th. Those days left the calendar. They did not leave the world — a clock running since 1701 ticked through every one of them.

So the charter instant is 9 Oct 1701 O.S. = 20 Oct 1701 N.S., and elapsed time must be measured from the second of those. Handing 1701-10-09 to a modern date library treats a label as an instant and overcounts by exactly 11 days. Measured properly, the charter was — ago.

The hour is not the hour either. The United States had no standard time zones until 1883; before that a town kept local mean solar time. New Haven sits at 72.9279° W and Eastern Standard is reckoned from 75° W, four minutes to the degree:

(75 − 72.9279) × 4 = 8 min 17 s

New Haven's clocks ran 8 minutes 17 seconds ahead of the zone that would eventually contain them. So a colonial 3:22 a.m. is about 3:13:43 a.m. by today's Eastern reckoning — the label is not the instant.

Which is the joke at this page's expense. The longitude correction is 8 m 17 s and the calendar correction is eleven days, while the offset this whole clock exists to model is five minutes. If you wound these dials in 1701, the errors you would have to fix first are larger than the effect you came to see.

The year, in Roman figures—

August is the eighth month to us and the sixth under the March-start year — which is exactly what Rome called it. It was Sextilis, the sixth, until the Senate renamed it for Augustus; July was Quintilis, the fifth, until it was renamed for Caesar. Two emperors broke the sequence, and the four months at the end kept their numbers and lost their places.

The Julian Day Number is the escape from all of it: a plain count of days since 1 January 4713 BC, with no months, no years, and no reforms. It is why the charter has one number under both calendars. 9 Oct 1701 Julian and 20 Oct 1701 Gregorian are both JDN 2342630 — the same day, counted twice by two systems that disagree about what to call it. Subtract the numbers and the eleven days take care of themselves.

The year sets in Roman figures without trouble. The Julian Day does not: 2,461,270 would want two and a half thousand M's, since the system has no symbol above one thousand. A notation that cannot count past its own largest unit is a notation with an horizon — which is the same complaint as a day that will not divide by fifty-five.
